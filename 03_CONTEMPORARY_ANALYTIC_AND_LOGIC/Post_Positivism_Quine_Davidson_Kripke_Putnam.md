### Question 1: Quine's Critique of the Analytic-Synthetic Dichotomy
**Format**: Conceptual Multiple-Choice (Advanced)  
**Topic**: W.V.O. Quine, "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" (1951) — The Circle of Analyticity

In Section 1 through 4 of "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" (1951), W.V.O. Quine challenges the foundational empiricist dogma that a sharp boundary exists between analytic statements (grounded purely in meanings independently of matters of fact) and synthetic statements (grounded in empirical fact). What is Quine's core argument demonstrating the logical circularity underlying every attempt to define the second class of analytic statements (such as "No bachelor is married")?

A. Such statements depend on psychological associations in the minds of speakers, which vary across socio-linguistic communities and therefore lack stable logical form.  
B. Second-class analytic statements can only be converted into first-class logical truths (such as "No unmarried man is married") by appealing to cognitive synonymy, but cognitive synonymy can only be non-circularly explained through interchangeability *salva veritate* in modal contexts (necessity), which itself already presupposes an antecedent grasp of analyticity.  
C. The distinction is refuted because empirical observation in quantum mechanics reveals that the Law of Excluded Middle fails, rendering all mathematical truths purely synthetic *a posteriori*.  
D. Quine argues that definitions in formal lexicons are prescriptive rather than descriptive, meaning that lexicographers determine truth-values arbitrarily by semantic fiat.

**Correct Answer**: **B**

#### Philosophical Grounding & Demonstration:
Quine distinguishes between two classes of analytic statements:
1. First-class: Formally logical truths, such as "No unmarried man is married." These remain true under any reinterpretations of their non-logical components and are governed strictly by logical particles.
2. Second-class: Statements like "No bachelor is married." These are not formal logical truths on their face; their truth allegedly depends on the "meaning" of terms.

To explain why "No bachelor is married" is analytic, traditional empiricists appeal to the claim that "bachelor" is *defined* as "unmarried man," or that the two expressions are *cognitively synonymous*. Quine systematically investigates every candidate explanation of cognitive synonymy:
- **Appeal to Definition**: Definitions given in dictionaries merely report preexisting historical usage; they rely on an already established synonymy rather than grounding it. Explicit stipulative definitions create synonymy only within artificial formal systems, which does not illuminate ordinary language.
- **Appeal to Interchangeability (*salva veritate*)**: Can synonymy be defined as the ability of two terms to be substituted for one another in all sentences without altering the truth-value? In an extensional language, terms like "creature with a heart" (*cordate*) and "creature with kidneys" (*renate*) might be co-extensional and interchangeable *salva veritate*, yet they are not synonymous. If one introduces an intensional adverb like "necessarily" (asserting that "Necessarily, all and only bachelors are bachelors"), interchangeability *salva veritate* does pick out synonymy; but the notion of necessity itself makes sense only if one already understands analyticity.
- **Appeal to Semantical Rules**: Positing artificial formal languages with explicit semantical rules merely labels certain sentences "analytic in language $L$"; it tells us which sentences belong to a list, but fails to explain what the general predicate "analytic" actually means.

Thus, Quine reveals an unbroken explanatory circle: Analyticity $\to$ Meaning $\to$ Synonymy $\to$ Definition $\to$ Necessity $\to$ Analyticity. None of these concepts can be explained without taking one of the others for granted.

#### Distractor Analysis:
- **A is incorrect**: Quine does not reduce analyticity to psychological associations; Quine is a committed naturalist and behavioral physicalist who specifically critiques mentalism and psychological notions of meaning.
- **C is incorrect**: While Quine later mentions that quantum mechanics might prompt revisions to logical laws (like the distributive law), this is part of his confirmation holism in Section 6, not his circularity demonstration regarding synonymy in Sections 1–4.
- **D is incorrect**: Quine explicitly states that dictionary definitions are descriptive empirical reports of prior linguistic synonymy, not arbitrary fiats, which is precisely why they cannot provide an independent philosophical foundation for analyticity.

**Primary Source**: W.V.O. Quine, "Two Dogmas of Empiricism", *The Philosophical Review*, 60 (1951), pp. 20–43; reprinted in *From a Logical Point of View* (Harvard University Press, 1953, ch. 2).

### Question 2: Quine's Confirmation Holism and the Web of Belief
**Format**: Conceptual Multiple-Choice (Advanced)  
**Topic**: W.V.O. Quine, *Two Dogmas of Empiricism* (§6) & *Word and Object* (1960)

In his critique of the second dogma of empiricism—**Radical Reductionism**—Quine formulates his celebrated thesis of **Confirmation Holism** (the "Web of Belief" or the Duhem-Quine thesis). Which of the following formulations most precisely expresses the epistemological consequence of Quinean holism regarding the status of logic, mathematics, and empirical recalcitrance?

A. Empirical statements encounter experience one by one in isolation, but formal logic stands in a transcendent metaphysical position immune from empirical revision.  
B. The totality of our knowledge forms a single interconnected fabric or field of force that impinges on experience only along its periphery; when recalcitrant experiences occur at the sensory periphery, adjustments can be made anywhere within the system, meaning that no statement is immune to revision (not even the laws of logic), and any statement can be held true come what may if sufficient adjustments are made elsewhere.  
C. Every scientific theory can be conclusively translated term-by-term into immediate sensory protocol statements, provided that higher-order set theory is utilized as the reduction base.  
D. Holism implies that scientific change is entirely irrational and subjective, because without an analytic-synthetic divide, empirical evidence has no bearing on factual belief.

**Correct Answer**: **B**

#### Philosophical Grounding & Demonstration:
The second dogma identified by Quine is reductionism: the belief that each meaningful empirical statement can be isolated and translated into direct reports of sensory experience (as attempted in Carnap's *Aufbau*). Quine counters this with confirmation holism, drawing on the insight of Pierre Duhem:
1. **The Unit of Empirical Significance**: The fundamental unit of empirical meaning is not the isolated sentence or single term, but the whole of interconnected science (*total science*).
2. **The Web of Belief**: Our conceptual system is envisioned as an interconnected web or field of force whose boundary conditions are sensory experience. The sentences closest to the periphery are direct observational reports (e.g., "There is a white patch"). The sentences at the interior core are theoretical generalizations, fundamental physical constants, and finally, at the deep center, the laws of logic and mathematics.
3. **Recalcitrant Experience and Redistribution**: When an empirical prediction fails at the periphery, our system of beliefs conflicts with experience. However, logic dictates only that *some* sentence in the vast conjunction of premises must be false; it never dictates *which* specific sentence must be abandoned.
4. **Universal Revisability**: Consequently, no statement is completely immune to revision. Even classical logical laws (such as the Law of Excluded Middle or the Distributive Law in quantum logic) may be abandoned if doing so simplifies the overall web. Conversely, any statement can be preserved as true "come what may" if we choose to make drastic revisions elsewhere in the web (for example, by positing hallucinations or amending physical laws). Pragmatic considerations—such as the "maxim of minimum mutilation"—guide how we redistribute truth-values across the web.

#### Distractor Analysis:
- **A is incorrect**: This is the traditional logical positivist view that Quine directly refutes; Quine explicitly denies that logic is transcendentally immune from empirical revision.
- **C is incorrect**: This describes Carnap's radical reductionist project in *Der logische Aufbau der Welt*, which Quine explicitly critiques as a failure.
- **D is incorrect**: Quine does not abandon objective empirical constraint; rather, he reconfigures justification as a holistic, pragmatic balancing of the web under the friction of sensory stimulation.

**Primary Source**: W.V.O. Quine, "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" (1951, §6); *Word and Object* (MIT Press, 1960).

### Question 3: Quine on Indeterminacy of Translation and Inscrutability of Reference
**Format**: Assertion-Reason (A/R)  
**Topic**: W.V.O. Quine, *Word and Object* (1960, Chapter 2) — The Radical Translation Scenario

**Assertion (A)**: In his radical translation thought experiment, Quine maintains that when a field linguist observes native speakers utter *"Gavagai!"* in the presence of a rabbit, there is no objective, factual matter of the dogmatic sort that settles whether the term refers to the whole rabbit, an undetached rabbit part, or a temporal slice of a rabbit (*inscrutability of reference*).  
**Reason (R)**: The indeterminacy of translation is merely an epistemic limitation arising from our temporary ignorance of the speakers' underlying neurophysiological brain states and private psychological meanings.

A. Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct philosophical justification for (A).  
B. Both (A) and (R) are true, but (R) is not the correct justification for (A).  
C. (A) is true, but (R) is false because Quine's indeterminacy thesis is strictly ontological/physicalist rather than a matter of temporary epistemic ignorance.  
D. (A) is false because stimulus meanings completely and uniquely fix singular reference; (R) is true.

**Correct Answer**: **C**

#### Philosophical Grounding & Demonstration:
In Chapter 2 of *Word and Object* (1960), Quine introduces the scenario of **Radical Translation**: translating an entirely alien language from scratch with no historical or cultural ties.
- When a rabbit scurries by, the native utters *"Gavagai!"* The linguist tests the **stimulus meaning**—the class of all sensory stimulations that prompt assent or dissent from the native. The stimulus meaning of *"Gavagai"* coincides perfectly with the stimulus meaning of the English word "Rabbit".
- However, as Quine demonstrates, matching stimulus meanings at the level of observational observation sentences does not fix the **reference** of the constituent term. Does *"Gavagai"* refer to:
  1. An individual rabbit (*a whole enduring substance*)?
  2. An undetached rabbit-part (*a temporal or spatial slice*)?
  3. The universal "Rabbithood" (*a property instantiated*)?
- To distinguish among these, the linguist must translate the native language's apparatus of individuation, identity, and quantification (words for "is the same as", "an instance of", "another"). But this apparatus can only be translated via a set of **analytical hypotheses** constructed by the linguist. Quine shows that mutually incompatible sets of analytical hypotheses can be constructed—each of which is fully compatible with the totality of observable verbal behaviors and dispositions—yet which assign completely different extensions and references to terms.

Crucially, as encapsulated in the evaluation of Reason (R): Quine repeatedly insists that the **Indeterminacy of Translation** is NOT an epistemic dilemma! It is not that there is some hidden, determinate "meaning" in the native's soul or brain that the linguist cannot discover due to limited data. Rather, linguistic behavior exhausts all the facts there are about meaning:
"There is not even such a thing as an objective matter of fact to be right or wrong about." Meaning and reference are fundamentally indeterminate and relative to a chosen manual of translation (*Ontological Relativity*). Thus, (R) is demonstrably false because it reduces an ontological thesis of physicalism and behaviorism to mere inductive skepticism.

#### Distractor Analysis:
- Options A and B are incorrect because (R) commits the exact error that Quine vigorously warned against in *Word and Object* and *Ontological Relativity*: confusing semantic indeterminacy with inductive/epistemic fallibility.
- Option D is incorrect because stimulus meaning determines only observation sentences as wholes, leaving sub-sentential reference radically inscrutable.

**Primary Source**: W.V.O. Quine, *Word and Object* (MIT Press, 1960, ch. 2); *Ontological Relativity and Other Essays* (Columbia University Press, 1969).

### Question 4: Late Wittgenstein: Critique of Ostensive Definition and Meaning as Use
**Format**: Conceptual Multiple-Choice (Advanced)  
**Topic**: Ludwig Wittgenstein, *Philosophical Investigations* (1953, §§ 1–38)

In the opening sections of the *Philosophical Investigations*, Wittgenstein quotes Augustine’s *Confessions* to illustrate a traditional conception of language: that every word has a meaning, that words name objects, and that sentences are combinations of such names. How does Wittgenstein critique this "Augustinian picture" and its reliance on **ostensive definition** (pointing to an object while naming it)?

A. Ostensive definition is rejected because material objects do not possess stable essences, meaning that language must refer exclusively to subjective sensory impressions.  
B. An ostensive definition cannot uniquely determine the grammar or role of a word unless the hearer already masters the overall framework of the language game; pointing to a two-shilling piece can be taken as defining its shape, its material, its color, its monetary value, or its number.  
C. Ostensive definition fails because words can only be defined mathematically using axiomatic set theory.  
D. Ostensive definition is valid for concrete nouns, but invalid for psychological verbs because inner experiences are completely non-physical.

**Correct Answer**: **B**

#### Philosophical Grounding & Demonstration:
In *Philosophical Investigations* (§§ 1–38), Wittgenstein exposes the fundamental illusions of the Augustinian picture of language. In the Augustinian model, language acquisition begins by pointing to things and pronouncing words, as though the child already possessed a language and merely needed to attach sound tags to pre-conceptualized items.

Wittgenstein demonstrates that **ostensive definition** is not a self-sufficient foundation for language:
In § 28, Wittgenstein writes: "An ostensive definition can be variously interpreted in every case."
Suppose someone points to two chess pieces and says "This is called *two*." Does the hearer understand the number two, the shape, the wood, or the game piece?
Or consider pointing to a color patch: "This is called *sepia*." Can the person know whether *sepia* is the name of a color, a type of paper, a shape, a posture, or a measurement? They can understand the definition *only if they already know how the word is to be used in the language-game*—that is, if they already know that the speaker is answering a question about color!
In § 31, Wittgenstein famously uses the analogy of chess: pointing to the king and saying "This is the king" explains nothing to someone who does not already know what a piece in a game is, what moves are, and what the goal of a game entails.
Thus:
"Ostensive definition explains the use—the meaning—of the word when the overall role of the word in language is already clear." Meaning is not a mysterious object correlated with a sign; rather, "for a large class of cases... the meaning of a word is its use in the language" (*PI* § 43).

#### Distractor Analysis:
- **A is incorrect**: Wittgenstein does not retreat into subjective sensory impressions; in fact, the private sensory impression model is the very target of his later Private Language Argument.
- **C is incorrect**: Wittgenstein explicitly moves away from the formal, logico-mathematical idealization of language found in his own *Tractatus*.
- **D is incorrect**: Wittgenstein shows that ostensive definition fails to be self-sufficient even for ordinary concrete physical objects, not just psychological verbs.

**Primary Source**: Ludwig Wittgenstein, *Philosophical Investigations* (1953, §§ 1–43).

### Question 5: Late Wittgenstein: The Private Language Argument
**Format**: Conceptual Multiple-Choice (Advanced)  
**Topic**: Ludwig Wittgenstein, *Philosophical Investigations* (1953, §§ 243–258) — The Diary of 'S'

In *Philosophical Investigations* (§§ 243–258), Wittgenstein presents the **Private Language Argument**. He considers a hypothetical solitary diarist who writes down the sign "S" in a calendar every time he experiences a particular subjective sensation. Why does Wittgenstein conclude that a "private language"—a language whose words refer to what can only be known to the individual speaker and to his immediate private sensations—is impossible?

A. Because human beings are biologically incapable of remembering sensory experiences for longer than a few minutes without neurological recording instruments.  
B. Because in a purely private sphere where only the subject has access to the sensation, there is no independent criterion or standard to distinguish between *actually remembering correctly* and *merely seeming to remember correctly*; whatever seems right to the subject will be right, which means that here one cannot talk about "right" at all.  
C. Because all mental sensations are brain states, and since brain states are publicly observable by neuroscientists, privacy is physically impossible.  
D. Because sensations do not exist; behaviorism shows that pain is nothing other than observable behavioral outputs such as crying out or flinching.

**Correct Answer**: **B**

#### Philosophical Grounding & Demonstration:
In § 243, Wittgenstein defines a private language not merely as a language someone invents for themselves (like Robinson Crusoe keeping a journal using ordinary words), but as a language whose individual words "are to refer to what can only be known to the person speaking; to his immediate private sensations. So another person cannot understand the language."

In § 258, Wittgenstein presents the definitive thought experiment:
A person wishes to keep a diary about the recurrence of a certain sensation. He associates the sensation with the sign "S" by concentrating his attention on it, giving himself a kind of "private ostensive definition."
Wittgenstein asks: What does this ceremony achieve?
- To use a sign consistently over time according to a rule, there must be a distinction between **following the rule correctly** and **believing that one is following the rule correctly**.
- In the case of an alleged private ostensive definition, the subject has only their memory of the past feeling to check their current application of "S". But how does the subject verify that their memory is correct? In the public realm, a clock or a standard meter or another speaker can check our memory. But in the private realm, using memory to check memory is like "buying several copies of the morning paper to assure yourself that what it said was true" (*PI* § 265).
- If there is no objective, independent criterion outside the subject's own impression of correctness, then "whatever is going to seem right to me is right. And that only means that here we can't talk about 'right'" (*PI* § 258).
Without a distinction between being right and seeming right, the sign "S" has no normative grammar, no semantic content, and cannot function as a symbol. A private language is therefore a conceptual impossibility.

#### Distractor Analysis:
- **A is incorrect**: The argument is not an empirical-biological claim about the fallibility of human neuro-memory; it is a conceptual/logical analysis of the normative conditions required for rule-following and linguistic meaning.
- **C is incorrect**: This is an identity-theory physicalist objection. Wittgenstein's argument is semantic and philosophical, not an appeal to neuroimaging.
- **D is incorrect**: Wittgenstein explicitly denies that he is a behaviorist in *PI* § 304–307. He does not deny the reality of pain; he denies that pain functions as a private object named by private ostensive definition (the "beetle in the box" analogy, *PI* § 293).

**Primary Source**: Ludwig Wittgenstein, *Philosophical Investigations* (1953, §§ 243–265).

### Question 6: The Rule-Following Paradox and Kripkenstein
**Format**: Conceptual Multiple-Choice (Advanced)  
**Topic**: Saul Kripke, *Wittgenstein on Rules and Private Language* (1982) & *PI* § 201

In *Philosophical Investigations* § 201, Wittgenstein writes: "This was our paradox: no course of action could be determined by a rule, because every course of action can be made out to accord with the rule." In his influential interpretation, Saul Kripke reconstructs this as the **Kripkenstein Paradox** using the mathematical operation **"quus"** ($\oplus$), defined as:
$$x \oplus y = x + y \quad \text{if } x, y < 57, \quad \text{and } x \oplus y = 5 \quad \text{otherwise}$$
What is Kripke's "skeptical solution" to this paradox, and why does it reject dispositionalism as a "straight solution"?

A. Dispositionalism fails because an agent’s physical dispositions are finite and prone to systematic error, whereas meaning is normative (determining what one *ought* to do); Kripke's skeptical solution grounds meaning not in private mental or physical facts, but in the community's criteria of assertibility and mutual correction.  
B. Dispositionalism is accepted by Kripke as the only scientific solution, proving that rules are hardwired into the central nervous system by natural selection.  
C. The skeptical solution demonstrates that mathematics is fundamentally contradictory, vindicating radical mathematical intuitionism.  
D. Kripke proves that an individual isolated from birth on a desert island can construct completely stable mathematical meanings solely through internal introspection.

**Correct Answer**: **A**

#### Philosophical Grounding & Demonstration:
In *Wittgenstein on Rules and Private Language* (1982), Kripke frames the skeptical challenge:
Suppose I am asked to compute $68 + 57$. I have never added numbers this large in the past. I answer $125$. A skeptic challenges me: How do you know that in the past, by the symbol "+", you did not mean the function **quus** ($\oplus$)? If you meant quus, your correct answer now should be $5$!
The skeptic demands: What **fact** about your past mental states or physical behavior constituted your meaning *plus* rather than *quus*?

1. **Failure of the Straight Solution (Dispositionalism)**:
   The most common response is dispositionalism: "To mean addition is to be disposed to answer $125$."
   Kripke offers two devastating rebuttals:
   - **Finitude and Error**: Humans are disposed to make calculation errors when tired, and human dispositions are finite (there are numbers so enormous that my brain would burn out before answering). Yet addition applies to arbitrarily large numbers.
   - **Normativity of Meaning**: Crucially, dispositionalism conflates the descriptive with the normative. A disposition tells us what a person *will* do; but a rule tells us what a person *ought* to do. Meaning is fundamentally normative: if I mean plus, I *ought* to say $125$, regardless of what I am physically disposed to blur out.

2. **The Skeptical Solution**:
   Kripke points out that Wittgenstein offers a **skeptical solution** (analogous to Hume's solution to induction). A skeptical solution concedes that there is no solitary, introspective, or physical fact that constitutes an individual meaning plus in isolation.
   Instead, meaning is grounded in the **practices of the community**:
   - We replace truth-conditions with **assertibility conditions** (*warranted assertibility*).
   - An individual is evaluated by other members of the community who share the same linguistic training. When an individual's answers accord with the community's shared practices, they are accepted as a competent rule-follower. If they continually answer $5$, they are excluded from the game.
   Thus, rule-following is intrinsically social and public, which immediately implies the impossibility of a private language.

#### Distractor Analysis:
- **B is incorrect**: Kripke systematically demolishes dispositionalism in Chapter 2, showing that it completely fails to account for the normativity of meaning.
- **C is incorrect**: The paradox does not claim mathematics is contradictory; it targets the semantic foundation of rule-following across all language.
- **D is incorrect**: Kripke's reading explicitly concludes that an individual considered in complete isolation cannot be said to follow a rule, because there is no external wedge between "seeming right" and "being right."

**Primary Source**: Saul Kripke, *Wittgenstein on Rules and Private Language* (Harvard University Press, 1982, chs. 2 & 3).

### Question 7: Kripke on Rigid Designation and the Modal Argument
**Format**: Conceptual Multiple-Choice (Advanced)  
**Topic**: Saul Kripke, *Naming and Necessity* (1972/1980) — Critique of Descriptivism

In *Naming and Necessity*, Saul Kripke delivers a lethal critique of the classical **Descriptivist Theory of Reference** (held in different versions by Frege, Russell, and Searle's "cluster theory"). A central weapon in Kripke's arsenal is the **Modal Argument**. Which of the following accurately formulates Kripke's Modal Argument against descriptivism?

A. Proper names like "Aristotle" have different grammatical senses in different languages, proving that translation across possible worlds is impossible.  
B. A proper name is a **rigid designator** (designating the same object in every possible world where that object exists), whereas a definite description (e.g., "the greatest pupil of Plato and teacher of Alexander the Great") is non-rigid; it is a contingent fact that Aristotle taught Alexander, and we can meaningfully consider a counterfactual world where Aristotle became a merchant and never studied philosophy, meaning the name cannot be synonymous with the description.  
C. Definite descriptions are rigid designators, whereas proper names change their referents depending on the speaker's psychological state.  
D. Proper names designate objects necessarily *a priori*, whereas descriptions designate objects contingently *a posteriori*, making the two syntactically incompatible in first-order logic.

**Correct Answer**: **B**

#### Philosophical Grounding & Demonstration:
The Descriptivist theory of names asserts that the meaning (or reference-determining mechanism) of a proper name is given by a definite description or a cluster of descriptions associated with it by speakers:
$$\text{"Aristotle" means "the pupil of Plato and teacher of Alexander."}$$
In *Naming and Necessity* (Lectures I & II), Kripke presents three major arguments against descriptivism: the Epistemic Argument, the Semantic Argument, and the **Modal Argument**.

**The Modal Argument**:
1. **Rigid Designator**: A term is a rigid designator if in every possible world in which the object exists, the term designates that very object (and never designates anything else).
2. Proper names are rigid designators: When we use the name "Aristotle" to speak about counterfactual situations, we are talking about *that very man* (Aristotle himself), regardless of what counterfactual properties he might have had in that world.
3. Definite descriptions like "the teacher of Alexander" are almost always **non-rigid** (flaccid): in other possible worlds, someone else (e.g., Philip of Macedon or Theophrastus) might have taught Alexander, or Alexander might never have had a teacher at all.
4. Now evaluate the modal proposition:
   $$\text{"Aristotle might not have taught Alexander the Great."}$$
   This proposition is intuitively **true**: Aristotle could have chosen a different profession, died in infancy, or gone into trade.
5. However, if the descriptivist were correct and "Aristotle" were synonymous with "the teacher of Alexander", the sentence would translate into:
   $$\text{"The teacher of Alexander might not have taught Alexander."}$$
   On a de dicto reading, this asserts that an individual could teach Alexander while not teaching Alexander, which is a contradiction.
Because proper names rigidly designate the historical individual across all counterfactual scenarios while descriptions express contingent properties that the individual might have lacked, proper names cannot be synonymous with, or have their reference fixed by, definite descriptions.

#### Distractor Analysis:
- **A is incorrect**: The modal argument is about possible worlds semantics and counterfactual conditions, not cross-linguistic translation difficulties.
- **C is incorrect**: This completely reverses Kripke's definitions; proper names are rigid designators, while typical definite descriptions are non-rigid.
- **D is incorrect**: Kripke specifically decouples the modal category of *necessity* from the epistemic category of *a priori*, showing that neither names nor descriptions are purely "necessary a priori."

**Primary Source**: Saul Kripke, *Naming and Necessity* (Lectures delivered 1970; Harvard University Press, 1980, Lecture I, pp. 48–59).

### Question 8: Kripke on the Necessary A Posteriori and Contingent A Priori
**Format**: Conceptual Multiple-Choice (Advanced)  
**Topic**: Saul Kripke, *Naming and Necessity* (1972/1980) — Decoupling Epistemology and Metaphysics

Prior to Kripke's *Naming and Necessity*, philosophical orthodoxy (stemming from Kant and logical empiricism) largely treated the following pairs of concepts as co-extensive:
- **A priori** was identified with **Necessary** (and Analytic).
- **A posteriori** was identified with **Contingent** (and Synthetic).

How does Kripke philosophically decouple these concepts, and which pair of examples correctly illustrates the **Necessary *a posteriori*** and the **Contingent *a priori***?

A. Kripke demonstrates that necessity is an epistemic property concerning what can be known through pure reason, whereas the *a priori* is a metaphysical property concerning alternative possible worlds; Example 1: $2 + 2 = 4$ is necessary *a posteriori*; Example 2: "Water is wet" is contingent *a priori*.  
B. Kripke demonstrates that **Necessity** is an ontological/metaphysical property (concerning how the world could have been across all possible worlds), whereas **Apriority** is an epistemic property (concerning how a proposition can be known independently of experience);  
   - **Necessary *a posteriori***: "Water is H₂O" (or "Hesperus is Phosphorus") — true across all possible worlds because both terms rigidly designate the same entity/substance, yet discoverable only through empirical chemical/astronomical investigation.  
   - **Contingent *a priori***: "Stick S is one meter long at $t_0$" — known *a priori* by the person who fixes the reference of the metric unit by stipulative baptism, yet contingent because the physical stick could have been longer or shorter had the temperature varied.  
C. Kripke rejects both categories as metaphysical nonsense, arguing with Quine that all statements are contingent *a posteriori*.  
D. Kripke shows that necessary *a posteriori* truths are identical to analytic truths, while contingent *a priori* truths are identical to synthetic truths in Kant's sense.

**Correct Answer**: **B**

#### Philosophical Grounding & Demonstration:
In Lecture I of *Naming and Necessity*, Kripke untangles two fundamentally distinct philosophical axes that had been conflated for centuries:
1. **The Epistemological Axis (A Priori vs. A Posteriori)**:
   This concerns how knowledge of a proposition is obtained. If a proposition can be known to be true independently of any particular empirical experience, it is knowable *a priori*. If it requires empirical investigation of the actual world, it is known *a posteriori*.
2. **The Metaphysical/Ontological Axis (Necessary vs. Contingent)**:
   This concerns the modal status of the truth itself. A proposition is necessary if it could not have been otherwise—that is, if it is true in all possible worlds. A proposition is contingent if it is true in the actual world, but false in some other possible worlds.

**Decoupling the Axes**:
- **The Necessary *a posteriori***:
  Consider identity statements between two rigid designators: "Hesperus is Phosphorus" or theoretical identity statements like "Water is H₂O" and "Gold is the element with atomic number 79."
  Since "Water" and "H₂O" are rigid designators for the same chemical substance, if this statement is true in the actual world, it is true in *all possible worlds* where that substance exists. There is no possible world where water is not H₂O (a substance with the phenomenal properties of water but composed of XYZ would not be water!). Hence, the proposition is **metaphysically necessary**.
  Yet, humanity did not know this by armchair contemplation; it required empirical experimentation by Lavoisier and Dalton. Hence, it is known **a posteriori**.
- **The Contingent *a priori***:
  Consider the standard meter stick in Paris, stick $S$. Someone fixes the metric system by stipulating: "Let 'one meter' refer to the length of stick $S$ at time $t_0$."
  The person who makes this stipulation knows *a priori* that stick $S$ is one meter long at $t_0$, without needing to conduct a scientific measurement (they fixed the reference of "one meter" by that very stick).
  However, the proposition is **contingent**: stick $S$ is a physical object made of platinum-iridium. Had it been heated or subjected to tension, it would have expanded. It is not true in all possible worlds that stick $S$ is one meter long at $t_0$. Thus, a contingent truth is known *a priori*!

#### Distractor Analysis:
- **A is incorrect**: It reverses the definitions: necessity is metaphysical (possible worlds), while apriority is epistemic (ways of knowing).
- **C is incorrect**: Kripke's life work was the resurrection of essentialism and metaphysical necessity, directly opposing Quine's modal skepticism.
- **D is incorrect**: Kripke explicitly demonstrates that necessary *a posteriori* truths are *not* analytic, and that the Kantian grid fails to map these distinct dimensions.

**Primary Source**: Saul Kripke, *Naming and Necessity* (1980, Lecture I, pp. 34–39; Lecture II, pp. 53–56; Lecture III, pp. 116–144).

### Question 9: Putnam's Semantic Externalism and the Twin Earth Thought Experiment
**Format**: Conceptual Multiple-Choice (Advanced)  
**Topic**: Hilary Putnam, "The Meaning of 'Meaning'" (1975) — Linguistic Division of Labor

In "The Meaning of 'Meaning'" (1975), Hilary Putnam introduces his famous **Twin Earth** thought experiment:
Imagine Earth and Twin Earth in the year 1750 (before modern chemistry). Earth and Twin Earth are phenomenologically identical in every respect, except that the liquid called "water" on Earth is composed of H₂O, whereas the superficially indistinguishable liquid called "water" on Twin Earth is composed of a complex chemical formula abbreviated as XYZ. Oscar₁ on Earth and Oscar₂ on Twin Earth are exact physical, neurological, and psychological duplicates. What fundamental thesis in the philosophy of language does this thought experiment refute, and what is Putnam's conclusion?

A. It refutes psychological internalism (the thesis that psychological states determine meaning/intension, and that intension determines extension), demonstrating that "meanings just ain't in the head!" because the extension of the term "water" in Oscar₁'s mouth is H₂O while in Oscar₂'s mouth it is XYZ, despite identical internal mental states.  
B. It refutes realism, proving that chemical kinds are social constructs created by modern laboratories after 1800.  
C. It proves that Oscar₁ and Oscar₂ mean the exact same thing by "water" because in 1750 neither had explicit knowledge of molecular structures.  
D. It demonstrates that semantic reference is determined solely by the psychological intentions of the speaker, vindicating Gricean intentionalist semantics over causal theories.

**Correct Answer**: **A**

#### Philosophical Grounding & Demonstration:
Traditional philosophy of language (dating back to Descartes, Locke, Frege, and the early Carnap) rested on two core assumptions:
1. Knowing the meaning of a term is a matter of being in a particular **psychological state** (semantic internalism).
2. The meaning (intension) of a term uniquely determines its **extension** (the set of things in the world to which the term applies).

Putnam’s Twin Earth experiment shows that these two assumptions cannot both be true:
- In 1750, neither Oscar₁ (on Earth) nor Twin Oscar₂ (on Twin Earth) knew chemistry. When they thought about "water", they had the exact same mental images, sensations of thirst-quenching, ideas of clear, odorless liquid flowing in rivers, and identical brain states. Internally and psychologically, their states were indistinguishable.
- Yet, when Oscar₁ said "water", he referred to H₂O. If a glass of XYZ had been transported to Earth in 1750, Oscar₁ would have been mistaken had he called it water—it would merely have *looked* like water, but it would not *be* water.
- Similarly, when Twin Oscar₂ said "water", he referred to XYZ.
- Therefore, the extension of their words was different, even though their psychological states were identical!

Putnam draws two radical conclusions:
1. **Semantic Externalism**: Extension is not determined by internal psychological states. Extension is determined by the actual nature of the physical environment with which the linguistic community interacts, and by the **causal chain / historical baptism** tying the word to that natural kind. In Putnam's immortal words:
   *"Cut the pie any way you like, 'meanings' just ain't in the head!"*
2. **The Division of Linguistic Labor**: Everyday speakers do not need to know the microstructural essence of an entity (e.g., how to distinguish genuine gold from iron pyrite) to use the word meaningfully; they rely on experts within their linguistic community who possess the specialized criteria of recognition.

#### Distractor Analysis:
- **B is incorrect**: Putnam's position is **scientific realism** and **essentialism** regarding natural kinds; water is objectively H₂O regardless of historical or social labels.
- **C is incorrect**: Putnam explicitly rejects this option; he insists that even in 1750, Earth water was H₂O and Twin Earth water was XYZ, and their words had different extensions and meanings.
- **D is incorrect**: Putnam’s thought experiment is a direct attack on internalist/intentionalist models like Grice's, proving that external worldly facts anchor semantic reference.

**Primary Source**: Hilary Putnam, "The Meaning of 'Meaning'", *Minnesota Studies in the Philosophy of Science*, 7 (1975), pp. 131–193.

### Question 10: Sellars on the "Myth of the Given" and the Space of Reasons
**Format**: Conceptual Multiple-Choice (Advanced)  
**Topic**: Wilfrid Sellars, *Empiricism and the Philosophy of Mind* (1956)

In *Empiricism and the Philosophy of Mind* (1956), Wilfrid Sellars launches his renowned assault on the **"Myth of the Given"**, which served as the cornerstone of traditional empiricist foundationalism (from Locke and Hume to Russell and the logical positivists). What is the central epistemological fallacy Sellars diagnoses in the Myth of the Given?

A. Conflating non-conceptual causal impingements of the physical world upon our sensory organs with the normative, epistemic justification of a belief inside the "space of reasons."  
B. Believing that scientific theories can explain empirical facts without relying on religious dogma.  
C. Assuming that mathematical deduction can produce new empirical facts about the universe.  
D. Conflating the syntax of ordinary language with the semantics of first-order predicate logic.

**Correct Answer**: **A**

#### Philosophical Grounding & Demonstration:
Traditional empiricist foundationalism sought to stop the epistemic regress of justification by appealing to a foundational stratum of immediate experiences: "sense data," "raw feels," or "uninterpreted sensory givens." The foundationalist claims:
"I know there is a red object before me because I am directly given a non-conceptual, immediate sensory impression of red, and this immediate givenness requires no further justification while guaranteeing truth."

Sellars identifies this as a fatal philosophical confusion:
1. **The Causal Dimension vs. The Epistemic Dimension**:
   Having a sensation (a physical photon hitting the retina, triggering a neurological state) is a purely causal, natural event in the physical world. It belongs to what Sellars calls the **space of causes** (or the realm of natural descriptions).
2. **The Normative Space of Reasons**:
   Epistemic justification, by contrast, is intrinsically **normative**. To justify a belief is to justify a claim to truth, to provide reasons, to evaluate evidence, and to be held accountable for inferences.
   In Sellars’ famous dictum (§ 36):
   *"In characterizing an episode or a state as that of knowing, we are not giving an empirical description of that episode or state; we are placing it in the space of reasons, of justifying and being able to justify what one says."*
3. **The Incoherence of the Given**:
   The Myth of the Given attempts to make a non-conceptual, natural causal state (a bare sensation) do normative epistemological work (serve as a premise justifying a belief). But a bare physical impingement has no propositional structure; it cannot serve as a premise in an inference! An item cannot simultaneously be:
   - Completely non-conceptual and immediate (so that it requires no prior conceptual understanding), AND
   - A reason for holding a propositional belief (which requires conceptual categorization and inferential integration).

Therefore, immediate sensory "givens" cannot serve as the autonomous foundation of empirical knowledge. All observational knowledge is conceptually mediated and presupposes an antecedent mastery of a public conceptual framework.

#### Distractor Analysis:
- **B is incorrect**: Sellars is a staunch scientific realist who argues that the scientific image ultimately completes and rectifies the manifest image.
- **C is incorrect**: This is the traditional issue of synthetic *a priori* mathematics, not Sellars' critique of sensory foundationalism.
- **D is incorrect**: Sellars' concern is epistemology and the philosophy of mind, not formal symbolic syntax.

**Primary Source**: Wilfrid Sellars, *Empiricism and the Philosophy of Mind* (Lectures 1956; Harvard University Press, 1997, § 36).

### Question 11: Davidson on Truth-Theoretic Semantics and Radical Interpretation
**Format**: Multi-Statement Evaluation (MSQ / GATE style)  
**Topic**: Donald Davidson, "Truth and Meaning" (1967) & "Radical Interpretation" (1973)

Donald Davidson developed a revolutionary program in the philosophy of language by adapting Alfred Tarski's formal definition of truth (Convention T) into an empirical theory of meaning for natural language, coupled with the methodology of **Radical Interpretation**. Evaluate the following statements regarding Davidson's semantic program:

I. Davidson argues that a theory of meaning for a language $L$ does not require positing mysterious intensional entities (like Fregean senses or propositions); instead, it takes the form of a Tarskian truth theory generating T-sentences of the form: $s \text{ is true in } L \iff p$.  
II. In radical interpretation, the interpreter must simultaneously solve for two unknowns: what the speaker means by their words, and what the speaker believes.  
III. To break the circle between belief and meaning, the interpreter must deploy the **Principle of Charity**, which maximizes agreement by holding that the speaker’s beliefs are by and large true and logically coherent.  
IV. Davidson accepts Quine's indeterminacy of translation, but rejects Quine's claim that there are no objective facts about meaning.

Which of the following combinations is strictly correct?

A. I, II, and III only  
B. II, III, and IV only  
C. I and IV only  
D. I, II, III, and IV

**Correct Answer**: **A**

#### Philosophical Grounding & Demonstration:
Let us analyze each proposition under Davidsonian semantics:
- **Statement I is TRUE**: In "Truth and Meaning" (1967), Davidson argues that appealing to "meanings" as entities (Fregean *Sinne* or mental ideas) is completely unexplanatory. Instead, a theory of meaning should give the truth-conditions of every sentence in the language using a finite axiomatic base. He deploys Tarski's Convention T:
  $$s \text{ is true in } L \text{ if and only if } p$$
  where $s$ is a structural description of a sentence in the object language, and $p$ is a translation of that sentence in the metalanguage (e.g., "'Schnee ist weiss' is true in German iff snow is white"). By substituting a theory of truth for a theory of meaning, semantics is grounded purely in extensional logic.
- **Statement II is TRUE**: In "Radical Interpretation" (1973), Davidson addresses how an interpreter can construct a truth theory for a speaker from scratch. The interpreter faces an interdependence: What a speaker utters depends both on **what their words mean** and **what they believe**. You cannot infer beliefs without knowing meaning, and you cannot infer meaning without knowing beliefs.
- **Statement III is TRUE**: To break this deadlock, Davidson introduces the **Principle of Charity** as a necessary methodological condition:
  The interpreter must assume that the speaker is rational, that their beliefs are largely consistent, and that their beliefs are generally true of the shared environment (comprising the Principle of Coherence and the Principle of Correspondence). Charity is not an act of empirical benevolence; without it, we cannot attribute propositional attitudes or meaningful utterances to an agent at all.
- **Statement IV is FALSE**: Davidson does *not* reject Quine's ontological conclusion. Davidson explicitly embraces Quine's indeterminacy of translation (which Davidson calls the **Indeterminacy of Interpretation**). Davidson agrees that multiple incompatible truth-theories can account for the totality of linguistic evidence, and that there is no deeper, hidden "fact of the matter" that makes one true and the other false.

Therefore, Statements I, II, and III are correct, while Statement IV is false.

#### Distractor Analysis:
- Options B and D are incorrect because they include Statement IV.
- Option C is incorrect because Statements II and III are foundational tenets of Davidsonian radical interpretation.

**Primary Source**: Donald Davidson, "Truth and Meaning", *Synthese*, 17 (1967), pp. 304–323; "Radical Interpretation", *Dialectica*, 27 (1973), pp. 314–328; reprinted in *Inquiries into Truth and Interpretation* (Oxford University Press, 1984).

### Question 12: Epistemology: The Gettier Problem and Post-Gettier Responses
**Format**: Comparative / Doctrinal Matching (Advanced)  
**Topic**: Edmund Gettier (1963) and Post-Gettier Epistemological Responses

Edmund Gettier's famous 1963 three-page paper, "Is Justified True Belief Knowledge?", demonstrated that the classical tripartite analysis of knowledge ($S$ knows that $p \iff$ (i) $p$ is true; (ii) $S$ believes that $p$; (iii) $S$ is justified in believing that $p$) is insufficient. Match the **Post-Gettier Epistemological Theories/Responses** in **List-I** with their **Defining Formulations or Architects** in **List-II**:

| List-I (Post-Gettier Epistemological Response) | List-II (Defining Formulation / Architect) |
| :--- | :--- |
| **p. Causal Theory of Knowledge** | **1. Alvin Goldman: $S$ knows that $p$ if and only if the fact that $p$ is causally connected in an appropriate way with $S$'s belief that $p$.** |
| **q. Tracking / Sensitivity Theory** | **2. Robert Nozick: $S$ knows that $p$ iff $S$'s belief tracks the truth via two counterfactual conditionals: if $p$ were not true, $S$ would not believe that $p$ (Sensitivity); and if $p$ were true, $S$ would believe that $p$ (Adherence).** |
| **r. Defeasibility Theory** | **3. Keith Lehrer and Thomas Paxson: Justified true belief is knowledge if and only if there is no true proposition $d$ such that if $S$ were justified in believing $d$, $S$'s justification for believing $p$ would be defeated.** |
| **s. Virtue Epistemology (AAA Model)** | **4. Ernest Sosa: Knowledge is a form of cognitive achievement evaluated like an archer's shot: Accuracy (true belief), Adroitness (exercising cognitive competence), and Aptness (accurate *because* adroit).** |

Choose the correct matching code:

A. p-1, q-2, r-3, s-4  
B. p-2, q-1, r-4, s-3  
C. p-1, q-3, r-2, s-4  
D. p-4, q-2, r-1, s-3  

**Correct Answer**: **A**

#### Philosophical Grounding & Demonstration:
- **p matches 1 (Causal Theory of Knowledge)**: Alvin Goldman published "A Causal Theory of Knowing" (*The Journal of Philosophy*, 1967). In Gettier's Smith-and-Jones case, Smith's belief that "The man who will get the job has 10 coins in his pocket" was accidentally true because Smith happened to have 10 coins in his pocket, but the fact that Smith had 10 coins had no causal connection with Smith's belief. Goldman argued that knowledge requires an unbroken causal chain linking the fact to the believer's belief.
- **q matches 2 (Tracking Theory / Subjunctive Conditionals)**: In *Philosophical Explanations* (1981), Robert Nozick introduced modal truth-tracking conditions to replace the traditional justification condition:
  1. $p$ is true.
  2. $S$ believes that $p$.
  3. **Sensitivity**: If $p$ were false, $S$ would not believe that $p$ ($\neg p \mathbin{\Box\rightarrow} \neg B(p)$).
  4. **Adherence**: If $p$ were true in close possible worlds, $S$ would believe that $p$ ($p \mathbin{\Box\rightarrow} B(p)$).
- **r matches 3 (Defeasibility Theory)**: Developed by Keith Lehrer, Thomas Paxson, and Peter Klein. The core thesis is that justification must be undefeated. In Gettier cases, there exists a true statement (e.g., "Jones will not get the job") which, if added to the agent's belief system, would instantly destroy the justification for the target belief. Knowledge is therefore *undefeated justified true belief*.
- **s matches 4 (Virtue Epistemology / The AAA Model)**: Ernest Sosa (in *A Virtue Epistemology*, 2007) resolved Gettier-type problems by modeling knowledge on the performance of a skilled archer:
  - **Accurate**: The arrow hits the target (the belief is true).
  - **Adroit**: The shot manifests skill and competence (the belief is formed via a reliable cognitive faculty).
  - **Apt**: The arrow hits the target *because* it was adroitly shot (the belief is true *because* of the agent's competence, not by epistemic luck). In Gettier cases, the belief is accurate and adroit, but it is not *apt* because the truth was a matter of accident rather than skill.

Thus, the exact correspondence is **p-1, q-2, r-3, s-4**.

#### Distractor Analysis:
- Options B, C, and D scramble the theoretical definitions and their historic architects:
  - In B, Goldman is attributed the tracking theory and Nozick the causal theory.
  - In C, Nozick is matched to defeasibility.
  - In D, Sosa is matched to the causal theory.

**Primary Sources**:
- E. Gettier, "Is Justified True Belief Knowledge?", *Analysis*, 23(6), 1963, pp. 121–123.
- A. Goldman, "A Causal Theory of Knowing", *The Journal of Philosophy*, 64(12), 1967.
- R. Nozick, *Philosophical Explanations* (Harvard University Press, 1981).
- K. Lehrer and T. Paxson, "Knowledge: Undefeated Justified True Belief", *The Journal of Philosophy*, 1969.
- E. Sosa, *A Virtue Epistemology: Apt Belief and Reflective Knowledge* (Oxford University Press, 2007).
