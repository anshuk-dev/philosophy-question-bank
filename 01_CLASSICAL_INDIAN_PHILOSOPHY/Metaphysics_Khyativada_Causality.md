### Question 1 (Format A: Conceptual MCQ - Sāṁkhya Satkāryavāda vs. Nyāya Asatkāryavāda)

In *Sāṁkhyakārikā* (Kārikā 9), Īśvarakṛṣṇa formulates five classic arguments to establish *Satkāryavāda* (the pre-existence of the effect in the material cause prior to its production):
$$\text{"asad-akaraṇāt upādāna-grahaṇāt sarva-sambhavābhāvāt}$$
$$\text{śaktasya śakya-karaṇāt kāraṇa-bhāvāc ca sat-kāryam."}$$

Consider the argument from **śaktasya śakya-karaṇāt** ("a potent cause produces only that for which it is potent"). How does this specific argument structurally undermine the Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika doctrine of *Asatkāryavāda* (*Ārambhavāda*), according to the Sāṁkhya commentators (Vācaspati Miśra in *Tattvakaumudī* and Gauḍapāda)?

A) If the effect were entirely non-existent (*asat*) prior to production, the causal potency (*śakti*) inherent in the cause could have no ontological relation (*sambandha*) with that non-entity, because a real potency cannot relate to an absolute void; thus, oil could as easily be pressed from sand as from sesame seeds.  
B) Because causal potency is identical with the divine will of Īśvara, and an omnipotent God cannot act upon a non-existent effect without violating the law of karma.  
C) Because the effect is produced as a de novo real entity through the destruction of the cause, meaning that *śakti* belongs only to the destruction (*dhvaṁsa*), which Sāṁkhya identifies as a positive substance.  
D) Because *śaktasya śakya-karaṇāt* proves that the cause and the effect exist in two entirely different moments of time, precluding any simultaneous causal contact (*sahabhāva*).

---

#### Correct Answer:
**A) If the effect were entirely non-existent (asat) prior to production, the causal potency (śakti) inherent in the cause could have no ontological relation (sambandha) with that non-entity, because a real potency cannot relate to an absolute void; thus, oil could as easily be pressed from sand as from sesame seeds.**

#### Philosophical Grounding & Primary Source:
In Īśvarakṛṣṇa’s *Sāṁkhyakārikā* 9 and Vācaspati Miśra’s *Sāṁkhyatattvakaumudī*, the argument *śaktasya śakya-karaṇāt* targets the ontological relationality of causal power. 

The Naiyāyika claims *Asatkāryavāda*—that the effect is an absolute non-existence before its causal generation (*prāgabhāva-pratiyogi*). But when the Naiyāyika is asked why oil is produced exclusively from sesame seeds and never from sand, the Naiyāyika replies that sesame seeds possess a specific potency (*śakti*) directed toward oil, which sand lacks. 

Vācaspati Miśra uncovers the fatal contradiction in this Nyāya defense:
Potency (*śakti*) is inherently relational; it is power *toward* something (*viṣaya* / *śakya*). Power cannot exist in a vacuum without an object to which it is directed. If the effect (oil) is non-existent (*asat*) prior to production, it is an absolute nothingness (*alīka*, like a sky-flower). A real causal potency existing in the seeds cannot enter into any ontological relation (*sambandha*)—neither contact (*saṁyoga*) nor inherence (*samavāya*)—with an absolute non-entity! If potency could produce an effect with which it has no relation, then anything could produce everything (*sarva-sambhavaprasaṅga*). Therefore, the effect must already exist in a latent, unmanifested form (*sūkṣma / avyakta rūpa*) within the cause, serving as the objective correlate of the cause’s active potency. Causal production is thus not creation *ex nihilo* (*ārambha*), but merely manifestation (*tirobhāva-āvirbhāva* / *pariṇāma*).

#### Distractor Analysis:
- **Option B is incorrect**: Classical Sāṁkhya (*Nirīśvara-Sāṁkhya*) explicitly rejects Īśvara as an active creator (*kāraṇa*); causality is entirely naturalistic, driven by the inherent teleology of *Prakṛti* for the sake of the *Puruṣa* (*puruṣārtha*).
- **Option C is incorrect**: Sāṁkhya never asserts that the effect is produced through the destruction of the cause; that is the Buddhist view of *kṣaṇikatvavāda* / *pratītyasamutpāda* (seed must perish for the sprout to emerge). For Sāṁkhya, the underlying *dravya* (the material substrate) is conserved eternally.
- **Option D is incorrect**: The argument *śaktasya śakya-karaṇāt* establishes the essential identity (*tādātmya*) and continuity between cause and effect (*kāraṇa-bhāvāt*), rather than temporal alienation.

### Question 2 (Format A: Conceptual MCQ - Rāmānuja's Satkhyātivāda and Trivṛtkaraṇa)

In the *Śrībhāṣya* (on *Brahmasūtra* 1.1.1), Rāmānuja famously propounds the radical epistemological-metaphysical thesis of **Satkhyātivāda** (or *Yathārthakhyātivāda*), asserting:
$$\text{"yathārthaṁ sarva-vijñānam iti veda-vidāṁ matam"}$$
$$\text{("All cognitions, without exception, are apprehensions of the strictly real").}$$

How does Rāmānuja resolve the classical counter-example of the perceptual illusion of silver in mother-of-pearl (*śukti-rajata*) within the ontological framework of *Satkhyātivāda*?

A) By asserting that the silver is a mental modification (*cittavṛtti*) projected outward onto the shell by the power of karma.  
B) By appealing to the scriptural doctrine of triplication (*trivṛtkaraṇa* / *pañcīkaraṇa*), arguing that because all physical substances share common elemental constituents, real silver particles actually exist within the shell; perception becomes "erroneous" in practical life only when the minor silver element is mistakenly privileged over the predominant shell-element for pragmatic transactions (*vyavahāra*).  
C) By demonstrating that the silver is an inexpressible (*anirvacanīya*) entity produced by ignorance (*avidyā*), which possesses relative empirical reality (*vyāvahārika-sattā*).  
D) By arguing that error is purely negative—a non-apprehension (*akhyāti*) of the difference between the perceived shell and the remembered silver—without any silver being present in the shell.

---

#### Correct Answer:
**B) By appealing to the scriptural doctrine of triplication (trivṛtkaraṇa / pañcīkaraṇa), arguing that because all physical substances share common elemental constituents, real silver particles actually exist within the shell; perception becomes "erroneous" in practical life only when the minor silver element is mistakenly privileged over the predominant shell-element for pragmatic transactions (vyavahāra).**

#### Philosophical Grounding & Primary Source:
In Rāmānuja’s *Śrībhāṣya* (1.1.1, *Mahāsiddhānta*), Rāmānuja defends the extreme realist thesis that whatever is perceived is objectively real (*sat*). To explain why someone perceives silver in a piece of mother-of-pearl, he grounds his epistemology directly in the Vedic cosmology of the *Chāndogya Upaniṣad* (6.3.3–4), which teaches *trivṛtkaraṇa* (the triplication of fire, water, and earth; later systematized into *pañcīkaraṇa*, the quintuplication of five elements). 

Because every physical object is an elemental mixture containing proportions of all other physical elements:
1. Every piece of mother-of-pearl contains a minute, real proportion of the elemental qualities and particles that constitute silver.
2. Under specific physical and visual conditions (defects in the eye, gleaming light, distance), the eye picks up the actual silver particles (*rajata-aṁśa*) present in the shell.
3. Therefore, the cognitive apprehension "This is silver" is not the apprehension of a non-existent entity (*asat*), nor an inexplicable entity (*anirvacanīya*), nor a mental idea (*ātmakhyāti*), but a perception of real silver objectively present in the locus!
4. Why then is it treated as an illusion in daily life? Because in that composite substance, the mother-of-pearl component (*śukti-aṁśa*) overwhelmingly predominates, while the silver component is too tiny to be manufactured into a piece of jewelry or sold for money. The cognition is called "erroneous" (*bhrama*) purely from the standpoint of practical utility (*arthakriyā-kāritva* / *vyavahāra-bādhā*), not because the cognitive content lacks ontological reality.

#### Distractor Analysis:
- **Option A is incorrect**: The projection of internal mental ideas onto the external world is **Ātmakhyātivāda**, the doctrine of Yogācāra Buddhism, which Rāmānuja forcefully refutes.
- **Option C is incorrect**: This is the **Anirvacanīyakhyātivāda** of Advaita Vedānta. Rāmānuja dedicates the *Saptavidha Anupapatti* (Seven Great Inconsistencies) precisely to destroying the Advaitic concept of an *anirvacanīya* entity produced by *avidyā*.
- **Option D is incorrect**: This is the **Akhyātivāda** of Prabhākara Mīmāṁsā. While Rāmānuja incorporates certain aspects of Prabhākara's rejection of positive misapprehension, he goes far beyond Prabhākara by asserting the actual ontic presence of silver particles via *trivṛtkaraṇa*.

### Question 3 (Format B: Assertion-Reason - Śaṅkara's Critique of Samavāya)

Given below are two statements: one is labelled as **Assertion (A)** and the other is labelled as **Reason (R)**:

**Assertion (A)**: In his commentary on *Brahmasūtra* 2.2.13, Śaṅkarācārya mounts a foundational critique against the Vaiśeṣika metaphysical category of inherence (*samavāya*), arguing that it leads to a vicious infinite regress (*anavasthā-dosa*).  
**Reason (R)**: If inherence is an independent, eternal entity distinct from the two relata it unites (e.g., substance and quality, or whole and parts), then this inherence itself, being distinct, requires a secondary relation of inherence to link it to the first relatum, and that secondary relation requires a tertiary one *ad infinitum*.

In light of the above statements, choose the correct option:
A) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).  
B) Both (A) and (R) are true, but (R) is NOT the correct explanation of (A).  
C) (A) is true, but (R) is false.  
D) (A) is false, but (R) is true.

---

#### Correct Answer:
**A) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).**

#### Philosophical Grounding & Primary Source:
In Śaṅkarācārya’s *Śārīrakabhāṣya* on *Brahmasūtra* 2.2.13 (*samavāyābhyupagamāc ca sāmyād anavasthiteḥ*), Śaṅkara rigorously deconstructs the Vaiśeṣika doctrine of causality (*Arambhavāda*) and category ontology. 

The Vaiśeṣika asserts that when a cloth is produced from threads, the cloth is an entirely new substance (*dravya*) that inheres in the threads by the eternal relation of *samavāya*. Similarly, qualities (*guṇa*) inhere in substances by *samavāya*.

Śaṅkara exposes the structural incoherence of this relational category:
1. The Vaiśeṣika insists that quality is entirely distinct (*atyanta-bhinna*) from substance, and the whole (*avayavin*) is entirely distinct from its parts (*avayava*). Because they are distinct, they cannot hold together without a real relational entity, which they name *samavāya*.
2. Śaṅkara responds: *Samavāya* is also completely distinct (*atyanta-bhinna*) from the substance and the quality it connects!
3. If distinctness (*bheda*) was the reason the quality required *samavāya* to relate to the substance, then *samavāya*, being equally distinct from both, must also require another relation (*samavāyāntara*) to connect it to the substance, and that another, generating a vicious regress (*anavasthā*).
4. If the Vaiśeṣika replies that *samavāya* relates itself directly without needing another relation (*svataḥ-sambaddha* / *svarūpa-sambandha*), Śaṅkara retorts: Then substance and quality, or cause and effect, can also be directly related without positing an artificial third entity called *samavāya*! 
Hence, (R) provides the exact philosophical and dialectical proof of (A).

#### Distractor Analysis:
- **Option B is incorrect**: (R) is not merely another true claim; it is the exact syllogistic core of Śaṅkara’s dialectical refutation in *Brahmasūtra-bhāṣya* 2.2.13.
- **Option C is incorrect**: (R) accurately captures the text of *Brahmasūtra* 2.2.13.
- **Option D is incorrect**: (A) is one of the most celebrated polemical arguments in classical Indian philosophy.

### Question 4 (Format C: Multi-Statement MSQ - Vaiśeṣika Atomic Theory: Pīlupāka vs. Piṭharapāka)

In classical Indian metaphysics, the transformation of qualities (such as color, taste, and smell) in physical substances through the action of heat/fire (*tejas*) forms the celebrated debate between the **Vaiśeṣika** doctrine of **Pīlupākavāda** and the **Nyāya** doctrine of **Piṭharapākavāda**.

Examine the following statements regarding the metaphysical mechanisms of these two theories:

I. According to the Vaiśeṣika doctrine of *Pīlupāka*, when an unbaked black earthen pot is baked in a kiln, the intense fire-contact (*agnisaṁyoga*) disintegrates the pot down to its ultimate constituents—the atoms (*pīlu* / *paramāṇu*); the heat then destroys the original black color in the isolated atoms and produces a red color, after which the atoms recombine via dyads (*dvyaṇuka*) and triads (*tryaṇuka*) to reconstruct a brand new red pot.  
II. According to the Nyāya doctrine of *Piṭharapāka*, the pot (*piṭhara*) is never destroyed into its constituent atoms during baking; rather, the fire particles penetrate the porous structure (*antarpraveśa*) of the unbroken, composite pot, changing the color of the whole simultaneously with its parts.  
III. The Vaiśeṣika admits *Pīlupāka* because it maintains that two mutually contradictory qualities (blackness and redness) can co-exist within the same composite substance (*avayavin*) provided they occupy different atomic layers.  
IV. The debate directly reflects their underlying ontology of the whole (*avayavin*): Nyāya emphasizes the structural stability of the composite whole, whereas Vaiśeṣika prioritizes atomic autonomy and the principle that qualities of the whole are strictly caused by the preceding qualities of its ultimate parts.

Which of the statements given above are strictly **CORRECT**?

A) I, II, and IV only  
B) I and III only  
C) II, III, and IV only  
D) I, II, III, and IV

---

#### Correct Answer:
**A) I, II, and IV only**

#### Philosophical Grounding & Primary Source:
In Praśastapāda’s *Padārthadharmasaṁgraha* (with Śrīdhara’s *Nyāyakandalī*) and Viśvanātha’s *Kārikāvalī* (verses 105–109):
- **Statement I is correct**: *Pīlu* means atom (*paramāṇu*). The Vaiśeṣika holds that fire cannot alter the intrinsic quality of an intact composite whole without first acting on the parts. The furious contact of fire causes molecular dissolution: it destroys the conjunction of atoms forming dyads (*dvyaṇuka*), destroying the dyads, triads, and the macroscopic pot. In the bare, disunited atoms, heat destroys the previous quality (black) and produces the new quality (red). Then, driven by unseen moral destiny (*adṛṣṭa*), the atoms undergo fresh conjunction (*saṁyoga*) to rebuild dyads, triads, and eventually a new macroscopic red pot (*rakta-ghaṭa*).
- **Statement II is correct**: *Piṭhara* means the composite pot/vessel (*avayavin*). The Naiyāyika considers the Vaiśeṣika view an absurdity that contradicts empirical recognition (*pratyabhijñā*: "This is the very same pot I placed in the kiln"). Nyāya argues that the pot remains structurally intact; fire-atoms (*tejaḥ-paramāṇu*) possess extreme subtlety and velocity, entering into the interstitial spaces/pores of the clay and baking the pot as an unbroken whole (*piṭharapāka*).
- **Statement III is INCORRECT**: Vaiśeṣika does **NOT** believe that contradictory qualities can co-exist in the same substance! In fact, the absolute impossibility of contradictory qualities (such as blackness and redness) co-existing in the same substrate is the *primary reason* Vaiśeṣika insists on the complete destruction of the old black pot before the red pot can come into existence!
- **Statement IV is correct**: The doctrine of *Pīlupāka* rigorously enforces the Vaiśeṣika causal law that qualities in an effect (*kāryaguṇa*) must be preceded by and generated from corresponding qualities in its inherent causes (*kāraṇaguṇa-pūrvaka*).

#### Distractor Analysis:
- Since Statement III contains a blatant misrepresentation of Vaiśeṣika ontology (Vaiśeṣika strictly adheres to the law of non-contradiction regarding qualities occupying a single *dravya*), any option including III (B, C, and D) is invalid.

### Question 5 (Format A: Conceptual MCQ - Metaphysical Structure of the Four Abhāvas)

In the Vaiśeṣika ontology of negation (*Abhāva-padārtha*, as codified in the *Saptapadārthī* of Śivāditya and the *Tarkasaṁgraha* of Annambhaṭṭa), non-existence is bifurcated into mutual non-existence (*anyonyābhāva*) and relational non-existence (*saṁsargābhāva*), the latter being tripartite: prior non-existence (*prāgabhāva*), posterior non-existence/destruction (*pradhvaṁsābhāva*), and absolute non-existence (*atyantābhāva*).

Which of the following correctly pairs these four varieties of *abhāva* with their exact temporal boundary conditions (*kālika-viśeṣaṇa*) and their logical function in causality?

A)  
- *Prāgabhāva*: Beginningless and endless (*anādiḥ anantaś ca*); prevents the cause from producing an effect.  
- *Pradhvaṁsābhāva*: Having a beginning and having an end (*sādiḥ sāntaś ca*); identical with the emergence of the new effect.  
- *Atyantābhāva*: Eternal (*nitya*); defined as the absence of identity (*tādātmya-sambandhāvacchinna-pratiyogitāka*).  
- *Anyonyābhāva*: Non-eternal; defined as difference across space.  

B)  
- *Prāgabhāva*: Beginningless but having an end (*anādiḥ sāntaḥ*); serves as the indispensable non-inherent condition (*kāraṇa*) for the production of an effect.  
- *Pradhvaṁsābhāva*: Having a beginning but endless (*sādiḥ anantaḥ*); represents the irreversible destruction of an entity.  
- *Atyantābhāva*: Beginningless and endless (*anādiḥ anantaḥ*); relational absence across all three divisions of time (*traikālika-saṁsargābhāva*).  
- *Anyonyābhāva*: Eternal (*anādiḥ anantaḥ*); defined as the absence whose counterpositiveness is delimited by the relation of identity (*tādātmya-sambandhāvacchinna-pratiyogitākaḥ*).  

C)  
- *Prāgabhāva*: Having a beginning but endless (*sādiḥ anantaḥ*); marks the potency of the seed.  
- *Pradhvaṁsābhāva*: Beginningless and endless (*anādiḥ anantaḥ*); marks the death of the effect.  
- *Atyantābhāva*: Having a beginning and an end (*sādiḥ sāntaḥ*); marks local absence.  
- *Anyonyābhāva*: Subjective mental differentiation.  

D)  
- *Prāgabhāva*: Anādi and ananta; identical with Prakṛti.  
- *Pradhvaṁsābhāva*: Sādi and sānta; identical with Vikṛti.  
- *Atyantābhāva*: Pure illusion (*māyā*).  
- *Anyonyābhāva*: Inherence (*samavāya*).

---

#### Correct Answer:
**B) Prāgabhāva: Beginningless but having an end (anādiḥ sāntaḥ); serves as the indispensable non-inherent condition (kāraṇa) for the production of an effect. Pradhvaṁsābhāva: Having a beginning but endless (sādiḥ anantaḥ); represents the irreversible destruction of an entity. Atyantābhāva: Beginningless and endless (anādiḥ anantaḥ); relational absence across all three divisions of time (traikālika-saṁsargābhāva). Anyonyābhāva: Eternal (anādiḥ anantaḥ); defined as the absence whose counterpositiveness is delimited by the relation of identity (tādātmya-sambandhāvacchinna-pratiyogitākaḥ).**

#### Philosophical Grounding & Primary Source:
In Annambhaṭṭa’s *Tarkasaṁgraha* and *Dīpikā*:
1. **Prāgabhāva**: *Anādiḥ sāntaḥ prāgabhāvaḥ; utpatteḥ pūrvaṁ kāryasya*. The prior absence of a pot has no beginning in past time (*anādi*), but terminates the exact moment the pot is produced (*sānta*). In Nyāya causality, *kāryotpatti-mātre prāgabhāvaḥ kāraṇam*—the prior non-existence of an effect is an indispensable cause; if there were no prior absence of the pot, the pot would already exist eternally, rendering production impossible.
2. **Pradhvaṁsābhāva**: *Sādir anantaḥ pradhvaṁsaḥ; utpatty-anantaraṁ kāryasya*. When the pot is shattered by a stick, its destruction has a definite beginning in time (*sādi*), but that destruction can never be destroyed to bring back that exact numerical pot; hence it is endless (*ananta*).
3. **Atyantābhāva**: *Traikālikasaṁsargāvacchinna-pratiyogitāko 'bhāvaḥ atyantābhāvaḥ*. Absolute non-existence (e.g., the absence of color in air, or fire in water) is beginningless and endless (*nitya* / *anādi-ananta*). It is an eternal absence determined by an ordinary relation of connection (*saṁsarga*).
4. **Anyonyābhāva**: *Tādātmya-sambandhāvacchinna-pratiyogitāko 'nyonyābhāvaḥ*. Mutual non-existence (difference / *bheda*, e.g., "a pot is not a cloth"—*ghaṭaḥ paṭo na*) is characterized by the negation of identity (*tādātmya*). A pot is eternally not a cloth, making *anyonyābhāva* ontologically eternal (*nitya*).

#### Distractor Analysis:
- **Option A is incorrect**: It mischaracterizes *prāgabhāva* as endless (if it were endless, the effect could never be born!), mischaracterizes *pradhvaṁsa* as having an end (which would mean a destroyed object could revive spontaneously), and confuses *atyantābhāva* with *anyonyābhāva* by assigning *tādātmya* to the former.
- **Options C and D are incorrect**: They assign arbitrary, completely erroneous temporal limits and conflate orthodox realism with Vedāntic or Sāṁkhyan categories.

### Question 6 (Format B: Assertion-Reason - Sāṁkhya Teleology and the Lame-Blind Man Analogy)

Given below are two statements: one is labelled as **Assertion (A)** and the other is labelled as **Reason (R)**:

**Assertion (A)**: In *Sāṁkhyakārikā* 21, the initial cosmic conjunction (*saṁyoga*) between insentient *Prakṛti* and inactive *Puruṣa* is analogized to the cooperative alliance between a lame man (*paṅgu*) and a blind man (*andha*).  
**Reason (R)**: Śaṅkarācārya, in his *Brahmasūtrabhāṣya* (2.2.7), refutes this analogy by demonstrating that whereas both the lame man and the blind man possess conscious intelligence (*cetanatva*) capable of purposeful coordination and voluntary termination of their contract, *Prakṛti* is purely insentient (*acetana*) and *Puruṣa* is entirely passive (*akartṛ*), making purposeful teleological movement or subsequent liberation (*mokṣa*) metaphysically impossible.

In light of the above statements, choose the correct option:
A) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).  
B) Both (A) and (R) are true, but (R) is NOT the correct explanation of (A).  
C) (A) is true, but (R) is false.  
D) (A) is false, but (R) is true.

---

#### Correct Answer:
**B) Both (A) and (R) are true, but (R) is NOT the correct explanation of (A).**

#### Philosophical Grounding & Primary Source:
- **Assertion (A) is true**: *Sāṁkhyakārikā* 21 states:
  $$\text{"puruṣasya darśanārthaṁ kaivalyārthaṁ tathā pradhānasya |}$$
  $$\text{paṅgv-andhavad ubhayor api saṁyogastatkṛtaḥ sargaḥ ||"}$$
  The conjunction of *Puruṣa* (who has sight/consciousness but cannot move—like the lame man) and *Prakṛti* (who can move/evolve but lacks sight/consciousness—like the blind man) takes place for the double purpose of *Puruṣa* observing the transformations of *Prakṛti* (*bhoga*) and realizing eternal isolation (*kaivalya*).
- **Reason (R) is true**: In *Brahmasūtrabhāṣya* 2.2.7 (*pāṅgvandhavad iti cen na, tathāpi*), Śaṅkara launches an incisive critique against the coherence of Sāṁkhyan dualism. Śaṅkara points out that the analogy fails at its crucial metaphysical joint:
  1. Both the lame man and the blind man are conscious human agents (*cetana*). The lame man can issue verbal instructions ("turn left, walk straight"), and the blind man can consciously heed and execute them. They form an intentional partnership.
  2. In Sāṁkhya, *Prakṛti* is entirely insentient (*acetana*) and blind; it cannot receive or interpret instructions. *Puruṣa* is utterly immutable, inactive (*akartṛ*), and without desire or intentional will; he cannot guide or instruct *Prakṛti*.
  3. Furthermore, the lame and blind men can decide to separate when they reach their destination. But unconscious *Prakṛti*, having no self-awareness, could never know when to stop creating, while *Puruṣa*, being incapable of action, could never dismiss her. Thus, cosmic bondage would be perpetual.
- **Why (R) is NOT the explanation of (A)**: Assertion (A) presents the classical Sāṁkhya thesis and its internal poetic analogy. Reason (R) is the external, hostile Advaitic refutation of that analogy formulated by Śaṅkara. While both statements are historically and philosophically true, (R) does not explain why Sāṁkhya posits (A); rather, it refutes the validity of (A).

#### Distractor Analysis:
- Candidates often instinctively mark (A) when both statements are profoundly true. Recognizing that (R) is a Vedāntic counter-critique and therefore *not* the causal or conceptual explanation for why the Sāṁkhya authors formulated the analogy requires high-order meta-philosophical discernment.

### Question 7 (Format C: Multi-Statement MSQ - The Metaphysics of Vivartavāda vs. Pariṇāmavāda)

In Indian philosophy, the ontological relationship between cause (*kāraṇa*) and effect (*kārya*) bifurcates *Satkāryavāda* into two radically opposed schools: **Pariṇāmavāda** (real transformation) and **Vivartavāda** (apparent/illusory manifestation).

Evaluate the following four statements concerning this metaphysical divide:

I. According to Madhusūdana Sarasvatī’s *Advaitasiddhi* and Prakāśātman’s *Pañcapādikā-Vivarana*, *Pariṇāma* is defined as *satattva-anyathā-bhāvaḥ* (the transformation where the effect possesses the same ontological status/reality as the cause), whereas *Vivarta* is defined as *atasattva-anyathā-bhāvaḥ* (an appearance where the effect has an ontological status different from and inferior to the causal substrate).  
II. Advaita Vedānta upholds *Vivartavāda* from the standpoint of the absolute reality of Brahman (*Paramārtha*), but accommodates *Pariṇāmavāda* at the empirical level (*Vyavahāra*) regarding the transformation of *Māyā* / *Avidyā* into the elemental world.  
III. The *Brahmapariṇāmavāda* of early Vedāntins (such as Bhartṛprapañca and Yādavaprakāśa) was accepted by Śaṅkarācārya as the ultimate metaphysical truth, provided it was purged of the Sāṁkhyan concept of three gunas.  
IV. In Viśiṣṭādvaita Vedānta, Rāmānuja upholds *Brahmapariṇāmavāda* by asserting that Brahman transforms from the unmanifest causal state (*kāraṇāvasthā*), where Cit and Acit are in subtle form, to the manifest effect state (*kāryāvasthā*), where Cit and Acit are in gross form, without Brahman’s essential nature (*svarūpa*) undergoing any intrinsic change.

Which of the statements given above are strictly **CORRECT**?

A) I, II, and IV only  
B) I and III only  
C) II, III, and IV only  
D) I, II, III, and IV

---

#### Correct Answer:
**A) I, II, and IV only**

#### Philosophical Grounding & Primary Source:
- **Statement I is correct**: In classical Advaita texts (*Vedāntasāra* of Sadānanda, section 16; *Advaitasiddhi*):
  $$\text{"pariṇāmo nāma upādāna-sama-sattāka-kāryāpattiḥ;}$$
  $$\text{vivarto nāma upādāna-viṣama-sattāka-kāryāpattiḥ."}$$
  *Pariṇāma* occurs when the effect shares the same order of reality (*sama-sattā*) as its material cause (e.g., milk transforming into curd: both are empirically real, *vyāvahārika*). *Vivarta* occurs when the effect possesses an order of reality discrepant with/inferior to (*viṣama-sattā*) its underlying substrate (e.g., the snake, which is merely apparent/*prātibhāsika*, appearing upon the rope, which is empirical/*vyāvahārika*; or the world appearing upon Brahman, which is absolute/*pāramārthika*).
- **Statement II is correct**: Advaita is structurally nuanced. While Brahman never undergoes *pariṇāma* (Brahman is *nirvikāra* / immutable, hence world-creation *vis-à-vis* Brahman is strictly *vivarta*), the physical universe is admitted to be a real *pariṇāma* (transformation) of *Māyā* / *Avidyā*. Māyā is the *pariṇāmy-upādāna* (the transforming material cause), while Brahman is the *vivartādhiṣṭhāna* (the immutable substrate).
- **Statement III is INCORRECT**: Śaṅkarācārya relentlessly combats and rejects *Brahmapariṇāmavāda*! In *Brahmasūtrabhāṣya* (e.g., 2.1.14 and 2.1.26–28), Śaṅkara explicitly attacks the view that Brahman literally transforms into the world. If Brahman literally transformed, Brahman would be composite (*sāvayava*), non-eternal (*anitya*), and subject to mutability, destroying the Upanisadic proclamation of Brahman as partless and changeless (*niṣkalaṁ niṣkriyam*).
- **Statement IV is correct**: Rāmānuja’s *Śrībhāṣya* explicitly defends a qualified *Brahmapariṇāmavāda*. Brahman, having the conscious (*cit*) and unconscious (*acit*) beings as His inseparable body (*śarīra-śarīrī-bhāva*), moves from *kāraṇāvasthā* (where the cosmic body is in an unmanifest, subtle state, *sūkṣma*) to *kāryāvasthā* (where the body is manifested in gross diversity, *sthūla*). The modifications (*vikāra*) affect only the body (matter and souls), leaving Brahman's inner essential nature (*svarūpa*) eternally pristine and full of auspicious attributes (*kalyāṇa-guṇa*).

#### Distractor Analysis:
- Since Statement III attributes *Brahmapariṇāmavāda* to Śaṅkara (which is the exact heresy Śaṅkara spent his philosophical life demolishing), any choice containing III (B, C, and D) is fundamentally false.

### Question 8 (Format A: Conceptual MCQ - Ātmakhyātivāda of Yogācāra Buddhism)

In the taxonomy of *Khyātivāda*, the **Yogācāra (Vijñānavāda)** school of Buddhism propounds the doctrine of **Ātmakhyātivāda**. 

What is the exact philosophical formulation of error according to *Ātmakhyātivāda*, and how is it refuted by the realists (Nyāya and Kumārila Bhaṭṭa)?

A) Error is the perception of an absolute non-existence (*alīka*), refuted by the realists on the grounds that nothingness cannot cast a cognitive shadow.  
B) Error consists in the mind cognizing an internal cognitive form (*buddhy-ākāra* / *vijñāna-svarūpa*) as if it were an external, mind-independent physical object (*bāhyārtha*); realists refute this by arguing that if external objects did not objectively exist, the internal cognition could never possess the phenomenal form of "out-thereness" (*bāhyatva*), since an analogy cannot function without an authentic external archetype.  
C) Error consists in mistaking one external object for another external object, refuted by realists because Yogācāra denies the existence of the mind.  
D) Error is the sublation of the subject-object duality into pure void (*śūnyatā*), refuted by realists who defend the eternal soul.

---

#### Correct Answer:
**B) Error consists in the mind cognizing an internal cognitive form (buddhy-ākāra / vijñāna-svarūpa) as if it were an external, mind-independent physical object (bāhyārtha); realists refute this by arguing that if external objects did not objectively exist, the internal cognition could never possess the phenomenal form of "out-thereness" (bāhyatva), since an analogy cannot function without an authentic external archetype.**

#### Philosophical Grounding & Primary Source:
In Vasubandhu’s *Viṁśikā* and *Triṁśikā*, as well as Kumārila Bhaṭṭa’s *Ślokavārttika* (*Nirālambanavāda*) and Jayanta Bhaṭṭa’s *Nyāyamañjarī*:
- **Yogācāra stance (Ātmakhyātivāda)**: Consciousness is self-luminous and diversified by beginningless subliminal impressions (*anādi-vāsanā*). There is no mind-independent external world (*na asti bāhyo 'rthaḥ*). What appears in perceptual illusion (like a dream, or the illusion of silver) is merely the internal form of cognition itself (*ātmanaḥ khyātiḥ* / *jñānākārasya khyātiḥ*). Due to ignorance, this purely internal subjective idea (*antar-jñeya-rūpa*) appears externally projected (*bahirvat avabhāsate*).
- **Realist Refutation (Nyāya and Bhāṭṭa Mīmāṁsā)**: Kumārila and the Naiyāyikas seize upon the phrase *bahirvat* ("as if external"). They argue:
  1. If an external entity (*bāhya-artha*) had never been perceived or did not exist in reality, the very concept of "externality" (*bāhyatva*) would be completely meaningless. One cannot say something appears "like an external object" unless external objects are genuinely known to exist (just as no one can say "Devadatta looks like the son of a barren woman").
  2. If all cognitive appearances were purely subjective states, cognition could never display spatio-temporal restriction (*deśa-kāla-niyama*); a person would see pots and silver anywhere and everywhere at will.
  3. Therefore, cognitive forms reflect real, mind-independent entities, proving that error cannot be reduced to subjective internal ideas projecting outward.

#### Distractor Analysis:
- **Option A is incorrect**: The apprehension of absolute non-existence is **Asatkhyātivāda** (associated with the Mādhyamika).
- **Option C is incorrect**: Yogācāra famously asserts the exclusive reality of consciousness (*cittamātratā*); it certainly does not deny the existence of the mind, nor is *Ātmakhyāti* about mistaking one external object for another (that is *Anyathākhyāti*).
- **Option D is incorrect**: This conflates Yogācāra idealism with Mādhyamika dialectic.

### Question 9 (Format A: Conceptual MCQ - Dravya and Guṇa Relation in Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika)

In the Vaiśeṣika ontology of substance (*dravya*) and quality (*guṇa*), as articulated in Kaṇāda’s *Vaiśeṣikasūtra* (1.1.15) and Praśastapāda’s *Bhāṣya*, consider the doctrine of the **guṇa-śūnya-kṣaṇa** (the qualityless initial moment of a produced substance).

Why does the Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika system metaphysically insist that a newly produced substance (such as a jar or cloth) must remain completely devoid of any quality (*nirguṇa*) during the very first moment (*prathama-kṣaṇa*) of its emergence?

A) Because qualities are illusions generated by the mind, appearing only after sensory contact occurs in the second moment.  
B) Because the substance is the inherent cause (*samavāyi-kāraṇa*) of its qualities, and according to the law of causality, the cause must precede the effect in time; hence, the substance must exist first before it can serve as the causal substrate for the generation of its qualities.  
C) Because atoms lose their individual qualities upon combining, recovering them only after Īśvara breathes life into the triad.  
D) Because inherence (*samavāya*) is non-eternal and takes one temporal atom (*truti*) to activate its connective potency.

---

#### Correct Answer:
**B) Because the substance is the inherent cause (samavāyi-kāraṇa) of its qualities, and according to the law of causality, the cause must precede the effect in time; hence, the substance must exist first before it can serve as the causal substrate for the generation of its qualities.**

#### Philosophical Grounding & Primary Source:
In *Tarkasaṁgraha* (*Dravyalakṣaṇa*) and *Bhāṣāpariccheda* (verse 3):
- Kaṇāda defines substance as: *kriyāvad guṇavat samavāyi-kāraṇam iti dravya-lakṣaṇam*.
- But a critical metaphysical objection arises: If substance is defined as "possessing quality" (*guṇavattvaṁ dravyatvam*), does a freshly produced jar qualify as a substance at the moment of its birth?
- The Naiyāyika answers with supreme analytical consistency: A cause must temporally precede its effect (*pūrvavṛttitvam kāraṇatvam*). The jar (a substance) is the *samavāyi-kāraṇa* (inherent material cause) of its own qualities (such as its color, weight, and size).
- If the jar and its qualities were produced simultaneously at moment $t_1$, then the jar could not be the antecedent cause of its qualities!
- Therefore, at moment $t_1$, the jar is produced as a bare, qualityless substance (*nirguṇa eva ghaṭaḥ utpadyate*).
- At moment $t_2$, the qualities of the jar are produced, inhering in the jar that existed at $t_1$.
- To prevent the definition of substance from failing (*avyāpti*) at moment $t_1$, the definition of substance is technically refined in Navya-Nyāya as: *guṇavad-anyatve sati guṇasamavāyikāraṇatvam* or *guṇātyantābhāvā-samānādhikaraṇatvam* ("Substance is that which is the inherent cause of qualities, or that which is not the locus of an absolute absence of all qualities throughout time").

#### Distractor Analysis:
- **Option A is incorrect**: Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika is thoroughly realist; qualities are never mental illusions, but objective physical realities.
- **Option C is incorrect**: In Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika, atoms never lose their intrinsic qualities (e.g., earth-atoms eternally possess smell, etc.); Īśvara does not "breathe life" into inanimate substances.
- **Option D is incorrect**: *Samavāya* is an eternal (*nitya*) and single category in Vaiśeṣika; it does not "take time" to activate.

### Question 10 (Format D: Comparative Matching - The Six Classical Theories of Khyāti)

Match the six classical Indian epistemological-metaphysical theories of error in **Column I** with their definitive philosophical propositions in **Column II**:

| Column I (Khyātivāda) | Column II (Definitive Philosophical Proposition) |
| :--- | :--- |
| **P. Akhyātivāda** | 1. The object of error is an absolute non-being (*asat*), projected as being through conceptual construction (*vikalpa*). |
| **Q. Anyathākhyātivāda** | 2. Error is purely omission: the failure to grasp the distinction between a present percept and a recalled mental trace (*smṛti-pramoṣa*). |
| **R. Anirvacanīyakhyāti** | 3. Error is the presentation of the self/cognition's own internal form as an external independent entity (*antar-jñeyarūpaṁ tu bahirvad avabhāsate*). |
| **S. Satkhyātivāda** | 4. Error is apprehension otherwise: a real object existing elsewhere is perceived in the present locus through extraordinary contact (*jñānalakṣaṇa*). |
| **T. Ātmakhyātivāda** | 5. The object of error is an ontological reality; all cognitions are true because all physical objects contain particles of all other elements via quintuplication. |
| **U. Asatkhyātivāda** | 6. The object of error is neither real (*sat*), nor unreal (*asat*), nor both; it is an inexpressible positive entity sublated by right knowledge. |

Choose the correct matching code:
A) P-2, Q-4, R-6, S-5, T-3, U-1  
B) P-4, Q-2, R-6, S-3, T-5, U-1  
C) P-2, Q-4, R-1, S-5, T-3, U-6  
D) P-1, Q-6, R-4, S-2, T-5, U-3  

---

#### Correct Answer:
**A) P-2, Q-4, R-6, S-5, T-3, U-1**

#### Philosophical Grounding & Primary Source:
1. **Akhyātivāda (Prābhākara Mīmāṁsā)**: Non-apprehension of distinction (*vivekāgraha* / *smṛti-pramoṣa*) -> **2**.
2. **Anyathākhyātivāda (Nyāya / Bhāṭṭa Mīmāṁsā)**: Cognition of a real object as located elsewhere projected onto the present locus -> **4**.
3. **Anirvacanīyakhyāti (Advaita Vedānta)**: The apparent object is inexplicable as either real or unreal (*sadasad-vilakṣaṇa*) -> **6**.
4. **Satkhyātivāda (Viśiṣṭādvaita / Rāmānuja)**: All knowledge is of the real (*yathārthaṁ sarvavijñānam*) via elemental mixture -> **5**.
5. **Ātmakhyātivāda (Yogācāra Buddhism)**: Internal cognitive form perceived as an external object -> **3**.
6. **Asatkhyātivāda (Mādhyamika Buddhism)**: Apprehension of pure non-being / emptiness reified as an existent entity -> **1**.

#### Distractor Analysis:
- Any variation from **A (P-2, Q-4, R-6, S-5, T-3, U-1)** misattributes the cardinal definitions that have demarcated classical Indian epistemology for two millennia. For example, in code B, swapping P-4 and Q-2 confuses Akhyāti with Anyathākhyāti; in code C, confusing R-1 with U-6 confuses Anirvacanīyakhyāti with Asatkhyātivāda.
