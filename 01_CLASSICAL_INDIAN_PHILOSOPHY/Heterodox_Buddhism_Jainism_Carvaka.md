### Question 1 (Format A: Conceptual MCQ - Dharmakīrti's Kṣaṇikavāda and Arthakriyākāritva)

In the *Pramāṇavārttika* (Pratyakṣapariccheda), Dharmakīrti establishes the definitive Buddhist ontological criterion:
$$\text{"arthakriyā-kāritva-lakṣaṇaṁ sat"} \quad (\text{"Existence is defined strictly as causal efficacy"}).$$

Through what precise dialectical dilemma does Dharmakīrti demonstrate that if an entity is real and causally efficacious, it must be instantaneous (*kṣaṇika*), thereby refuting the eternalist (*nityatva*) metaphysics of the Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika?

A) If an entity were eternal, it would have to produce its effects either simultaneously (*yugapat*) or successively (*kramena*). It cannot produce them simultaneously, for then all its past, present, and future effects would occur in a single instant, leaving it causally impotent in subsequent moments; nor can it produce them successively, because an unchanging entity cannot produce different effects at different times without its causal capacity undergoing intrinsic change.  
B) If an entity were eternal, it would be identical with Īśvara, but since Īśvara is free from desire, He cannot have the desire to produce effects.  
C) Because eternal entities are perceptible, whereas causal efficacy is purely inferential, meaning that eternal entities cannot enter into sensory contact.  
D) Because the destruction of an entity requires an external hammer, and if an entity were eternal, the hammer would be destroyed upon impact.

---

#### Correct Answer:
**A) If an entity were eternal, it would have to produce its effects either simultaneously (yugapat) or successively (kramena). It cannot produce them simultaneously, for then all its past, present, and future effects would occur in a single instant, leaving it causally impotent in subsequent moments; nor can it produce them successively, because an unchanging entity cannot produce different effects at different times without its causal capacity undergoing intrinsic change.**

#### Philosophical Grounding & Primary Source:
In Dharmakīrti’s *Pramāṇavārttika* (II.1–6) and Ratnakīrti’s *Kṣaṇabhaṅgasiddhi* (*Anvayātmikā* & *Vyatirekātmikā*):
- The proof of momentariness is structured upon the syllogism:
  $$\text{"Yat sat tat kṣaṇikam, yathā ghaṭādiḥ; santas caite bhāvāḥ."}$$
  ("Whatever is existent is momentary, like a jar; and all these entities are existent.")
- The criterion of existence (*sattā*) is causal efficacy (*arthakriyākāritva*): that which cannot produce any effect is as unreal as a hare's horn (*śaśaviṣāṇa*).
- Ratnakīrti and Dharmakīrti subject the concept of an eternal (*nitya*) cause to the dilemma of simultaneity (*yugapad-bhāva*) versus succession (*krama-bhāva*):
  1. Suppose an eternal seed produces its sprout. Does it produce all its sprouts simultaneously (*yugapat*) or in temporal succession (*krameṇa*)?
  2. If it produces them simultaneously, then all sprouts across all time must be generated in the first moment. After that first moment, the seed would have nothing left to produce, rendering it causally inert (*a-kārakam*). But an entity that lacks causal efficacy is, by definition, non-existent!
  3. If the eternal seed produces its sprouts successively (at moment $t_1, t_2, t_3 \dots$), what accounts for this delay? If the seed at $t_1$ was fully competent to produce the sprout at $t_2$, it would have produced it at $t_1$. If it was not competent at $t_1$, it lacked the potency.
  4. If the opponent argues that the seed waits for auxiliary conditions (*sahakāri-pratyaya*, like soil and water), does the auxiliary condition impart an addition/change (*atiśaya*) to the seed or not? If it does, the seed has undergone change, ceasing to be eternal. If it does not impart any change, the seed remains identical, and the presence or absence of auxiliaries is irrelevant.
  5. Capacity to act (*sāmarthya*) and incapacity to act (*asāmarthya*) are mutually contradictory properties (*viruddhadharmādhyāsa*). If an entity possesses capacity at $t_2$ which it lacked at $t_1$, it is numerically different. Therefore, whatever produces an effect exists only for that single discrete point-instant (*kṣaṇa*).

#### Distractor Analysis:
- **Option B is incorrect**: The argument from *arthakriyākāritva* is an autonomous ontological and logical proof applied to all entities (inanimate seeds, physical atoms, mental states), not restricted to theological disputes about Īśvara.
- **Option C is incorrect**: In Buddhist epistemology (Dharmakīrti), *svalakṣaṇa* (the momentary particular) is precisely the *only* object that is directly perceived (*pratyakṣa-viṣaya*); eternal entities are mental fictions (*vikalpa*).
- **Option D is incorrect**: This is an absurd caricature. In Buddhism, destruction is uncaused and spontaneous (*ahetuko vināśaḥ*); things perish by their very nature of being born, not by the blow of a hammer.

### Question 2 (Format A: Conceptual MCQ - Sautrāntika Bāhyānumeyavāda vs. Vaibhāṣika Bāhyapratyakṣavāda)

Within the Sarvāstivāda stream of Early Buddhism, the **Vaibhāṣika** and **Sautrāntika** schools fundamentally split over the epistemological accessibility of the external world.

Which of the following statements correctly captures the precise epistemic reason why the **Sautrāntika** insists on **Bāhyānumeyavāda** (the indirect, inferential reality of external objects), and how it rejects the Vaibhāṣika doctrine of direct perception (**Bāhyapratyakṣavāda**)?

A) The Sautrāntika holds that external objects are mere illusions produced by karma, so they can only be inferred through scriptures.  
B) Because according to *Kṣaṇikavāda*, the external object that causes a perception perishes in the very moment of its causal operation ($t_1$); hence, by the time the sensory consciousness arises in the subsequent moment ($t_2$), the object no longer exists, meaning consciousness can only perceive its own internalized cognitive imprint/form (*jñānākāra*), from which the antecedent existence of the external object is inferred as its cause.  
C) Because the Sautrāntika rejects the authority of the *Abhidharma-mahāvibhāṣā* and accepts only the *Vinaya Piṭaka*, which prohibits the direct visual inspection of material forms.  
D) Because external objects consist of indivisible partless atoms, and partless atoms are by definition beyond the threshold of sensory apprehension (*atīndriya*).

---

#### Correct Answer:
**B) Because according to Kṣaṇikavāda, the external object that causes a perception perishes in the very moment of its causal operation ($t_1$); hence, by the time the sensory consciousness arises in the subsequent moment ($t_2$), the object no longer exists, meaning consciousness can only perceive its own internalized cognitive imprint/form (jñānākāra), from which the antecedent existence of the external object is inferred as its cause.**

#### Philosophical Grounding & Primary Source:
In Vasubandhu’s *Abhidharmakośabhāṣya* (Chapter 1 & 9) and Mādhavācārya’s *Sarvadarśanasaṁgraha* (*Bauddhadarśanam*):
- **Vaibhāṣika View (Bāhyapratyakṣavāda)**: The Vaibhāṣikas (following the *Mahāvibhāṣā*) hold that external objects exist substantially (*dravyasat*) across all three times (past, present, future: *sarvam asti*) and are directly grasped by sensory perception without the mediation of an internal cognitive image (*nirākāra-jñāna-vāda*).
- **Sautrāntika Critique and Bāhyānumeyavāda**:
  1. The Sautrāntika enforces the strict Buddhist law of momentariness (*kṣaṇikavāda*) and causality. A cause must temporally precede its effect.
  2. The external object (e.g., a blue flower) acts as the objective condition (*ālambana-pratyaya*) at moment $t_1$ to generate the visual consciousness (*cakṣur-vijñāna*).
  3. But according to universal momentariness, the external blue object perishes at the very end of $t_1$.
  4. The sensory consciousness arises at moment $t_2$. At $t_2$, the external object is already dead and gone!
  5. How can consciousness directly perceive an object that does not exist at the moment consciousness occurs? Direct contact (*pratyakṣa*) with a non-existent entity is impossible.
  6. Therefore, what consciousness directly experiences at $t_2$ is the cognitive form or impression (*ākāra*) stamped onto consciousness by the external object before it vanished (*sākāra-jñāna-vāda*).
  7. From this distinct cognitive form (*ākāra-viśeṣa*), we infer the prior existence of the external object as its material cause (*kāraṇa-liṅgaka-bāhyārtha-anumāna*). Hence: *Bāhyānumeyavāda*.

#### Distractor Analysis:
- **Option A is incorrect**: The Sautrāntika is a realist school; it does *not* claim that external objects are mere illusions or mind-only creations (that is the Yogācāra idealist stance).
- **Option C is incorrect**: Sautrāntikas are named after the *Sūtra Piṭaka* (not *Vinaya*), and their epistemic theories are derived from rigorous logical analysis of causality and momentariness, not monastic taboos.
- **Option D is incorrect**: While both schools analyze matter into atoms, the specific epistemic divergence of *Bāhyānumeyavāda* turns strictly upon the temporal gap between the causal moment ($t_1$) and the effect moment ($t_2$) in light of *kṣaṇikavāda*.

### Question 3 (Format C: Multi-Statement MSQ - Yogācāra Trisvabhāva & Ālayavijñāna)

In the *Triṁśikā-kārikā* and *Madhyāntavibhāga*, Vasubandhu and Maitreyanātha expound the foundational metaphysics of Yogācāra (Vijñānavāda). 

Examine the following four statements regarding the doctrine of the **Three Natures (Trisvabhāva)** and the **Storehouse Consciousness (Ālayavijñāna)**:

I. The *Parikalpita-svabhāva* (imagined nature) refers to the false imputation of the subject-object duality (*grāhya-grāhaka-bhāva*) and external mind-independent entities onto the continuous flow of consciousness.  
II. The *Paratantra-svabhāva* (dependent nature) represents the causal network of momentary conscious events (*cittacaittāḥ*) arising through dependent origination (*pratītyasamutpāda*), functioning as the locus of both delusion and purification.  
III. The *Pariniṣpanna-svabhāva* (perfected nature) is the complete and permanent absence of the imagined duality (*parikalpita*) within the dependent nature (*paratantra*), realizing Suchness (*tathatā*).  
IV. The *Ālayavijñāna* is an eternal, unchanging, transcendental Self (*ātman*) identical with the Advaitic Brahman, which permanently witnesses the six active sense-consciousnesses (*pravṛtti-vijñāna*).

Which of the statements given above are strictly **CORRECT**?

A) I, II, and III only  
B) I and IV only  
C) II, III, and IV only  
D) I, II, III, and IV

---

#### Correct Answer:
**A) I, II, and III only**

#### Philosophical Grounding & Primary Source:
In Vasubandhu’s *Triṁśikā-vijñaptimātratāsiddhi* (verses 20–22):
- **Statement I is correct**: *Yena yena vikalpena yad yad vastu vikalpyate, parikalpita evāsau svabhāvo na sa vidyate* (Kārikā 20). The imagined nature (*parikalpita*) is the conceptual reification of phenomenal reality into external objects to be grasped (*grāhya*) and an internal subject that grasps (*grāhaka*). It is purely nominal, lacking intrinsic reality.
- **Statement II is correct**: *Paratantra-svabhāvas tu vikalpaḥ pratyayodbhavaḥ* (Kārikā 21). The dependent nature is the dynamic stream of causally conditioned mental phenomena (*vikalpa* / cognitive events) emerging from underlying karmic seeds (*bīja*). It is called dependent (*para-tantra*) because it depends on antecedent causal conditions (*pratyaya*).
- **Statement III is correct**: *Niṣpannas tasya pūrveṇa sadā rahitatā tu yā* (Kārikā 21). The perfected nature (*pariniṣpanna*) is nothing other than the *paratantra* completely and eternally emptied of the *parikalpita* (the subject-object bifurcation). It is the non-dual ultimate reality (*dharmatā* / *tathatā*).
- **Statement IV is INCORRECT**: In Yogācāra, the *Ālayavijñāna* (storehouse consciousness) is emphatically **NOT** an eternal, unchanging Self (*ātman*)! Vasubandhu explicitly states in *Triṁśikā* (verse 4):
  $$\text{"tac ca vartate srotasaughavat"}$$
  ("And that [ālayavijñāna] flows continuously like a raging river torrent").
  The *Ālayavijñāna* is a dynamic, rapidly changing stream of momentary cognitive point-instants carrying karmic seeds (*sarva-bījakam*). At the attainment of Arhatship/Enlightenment (*āśraya-parāvṛtti*—the fundamental revolution of the basis), the stream of *ālayavijñāna* is completely transformed and extinguished. Equating it with an eternal Advaitic *Ātman* is a cardinal error in Buddhist scholarship.

#### Distractor Analysis:
- Since Statement IV commits the fatal error of eternalizing the *Ālayavijñāna* into an unchanging *Ātman* (violating the core Buddhist axiom of *Anātmavāda*), any option containing IV (B, C, and D) is definitively eliminated.

### Question 4 (Format B: Assertion-Reason - Nāgārjuna's Equivalence of Śūnyatā and Pratītyasamutpāda)

Given below are two statements: one is labelled as **Assertion (A)** and the other is labelled as **Reason (R)**:

**Assertion (A)**: In the *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā* (24.18), Nāgārjuna asserts the absolute philosophical identity between Dependent Origination (*pratītyasamutpāda*) and Emptiness (*śūnyatā*):
$$\text{"yaḥ pratītya-samutpādaḥ śūnyatāṁ tāṁ pracakṣmahe |"}$$  
**Reason (R)**: Because whatever entity originates through causes and dependent conditions (*pratītya yaḥ samutpannaḥ*) is intrinsically devoid of self-nature (*nihsvabhāva*); if an entity possessed an independent, unconditioned intrinsic nature (*svabhāva*), it could neither be produced, nor changed, nor destroyed, rendering causal interaction and spiritual transformation impossible.

In light of the above statements, choose the correct option:
A) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).  
B) Both (A) and (R) are true, but (R) is NOT the correct explanation of (A).  
C) (A) is true, but (R) is false.  
D) (A) is false, but (R) is true.

---

#### Correct Answer:
**A) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).**

#### Philosophical Grounding & Primary Source:
In Nāgārjuna’s *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā* (MMK 24.18–19 and 24.38) and Candrakīrti’s *Prasannapadā*:
- **Assertion (A)**: MMK 24.18 famously states:
  $$\text{"yaḥ pratītyasamutpādaḥ śūnyatāṁ tāṁ pracakṣmahe |}$$
  $$\text{sā prajñaptir upādāya pratipat saiva madhyamā ||"}$$
  ("That which is dependent origination, we declare to be emptiness. It is a dependent designation; it is indeed the Middle Way.")
- **Reason (R)**: In MMK 24.19, Nāgārjuna immediately provides the rigorous logical grounding for this equation:
  $$\text{"apratītya samutpanno dharmaḥ kaścin na vidyate |}$$
  $$\text{yasmāt tasmād aśūnyo 'hi dharmaḥ kaścin na vidyate ||"}$$
  ("Since there is no entity whatsoever that does not originate dependently, therefore there is no entity whatsoever that is not empty.")
  *Svabhāva* (intrinsic essence/nature) is by definition *akṛtrima* (unfabricated) and *paratra nirapekṣa* (independent of anything else). If a thing had *svabhāva*, it would exist eternally in its own self-enclosed reality; it could not depend on causes to be born, nor could it perish. Therefore, dependent origination is not the creation of things with intrinsic essence, but the very proof that all things are totally empty of intrinsic essence (*niḥsvabhāva* = *śūnya*). Thus, (R) provides the definitive logical demonstration of (A).

#### Distractor Analysis:
- **Option B is incorrect**: (R) is not a disconnected remark; it is the precise dialectical argument that bridges *pratītyasamutpāda* with *śūnyatā*, directly explaining Assertion (A).
- **Options C and D are incorrect**: Both statements are direct canonical foundations of Mādhyamika philosophy.

### Question 5 (Format A: Conceptual MCQ - Mādhyamika Catuṣkoṭi on Causation)

In the opening chapter of the *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā* (*Pratyayaparīkṣā*, MMK 1.1), Nāgārjuna presents the celebrated **Catuṣkoṭi** (fourfold tetralemma) negating all four possible ontological permutations of causal origination:
$$\text{"na svato nāpi parato na dvābhyāṁ nāpy ahetutaḥ |}$$
$$\text{utpannā jātu vidyante bhāvāḥ kvacana kecana ||"}$$

Which of the following correctly pairs each of the four rejected positions (*koṭis*) with the specific philosophical school holding that thesis, and Nāgārjuna's exact dialectical refutation of it?

A)  
1. Self-origination (*svataḥ*): Held by Sāṁkhya (*Satkāryavāda*); refuted because if the effect already exists within the cause, its production is redundant and meaningless (*janma-vaiyarthyam*), and production would never cease.  
2. Origination from another (*parataḥ*): Held by Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika (*Asatkāryavāda*); refuted because if an effect could be produced from an entirely other/alien cause, then thick darkness could be produced from a blazing lamp.  
3. Origination from both (*dvābhyām*): Held by Jaina metaphysics (*Anekāntavāda*); refuted because it merely combines the absurdities and contradictions of both self-origination and other-origination.  
4. Uncaused origination (*ahetutaḥ*): Held by Cārvāka / Svabhāvavāda; refuted because if things were uncaused, effects would arise anywhere, at any time, without restriction, or never arise at all.  

B)  
1. Self-origination: Held by Nyāya; refuted because atoms cannot combine.  
2. Origination from another: Held by Sāṁkhya; refuted because Prakṛti is blind.  
3. Origination from both: Held by Advaita; refuted because Brahman is non-dual.  
4. Uncaused origination: Held by Buddhism; refuted because Buddha taught karma.  

C)  
1. Self-origination: Held by Yogācāra; refuted because mind is empty.  
2. Origination from another: Held by Cārvāka; refuted because perception is flawed.  
3. Origination from both: Held by Vaibhāṣika; refuted because past and future exist.  
4. Uncaused origination: Held by Jaimini; refuted because Vedas are eternal.  

D)  
1. Self-origination: Held by Mīmāṁsā; refuted by Apurva.  
2. Origination from another: Held by Dvaita; refuted by Taratamya.  
3. Origination from both: Held by Kashmir Śaivism; refuted by Spanda.  
4. Uncaused origination: Held by Cārvāka; refuted by Lokāyata.

---

#### Correct Answer:
**A) 1. Self-origination (svataḥ): Held by Sāṁkhya (Satkāryavāda); refuted because if the effect already exists within the cause, its production is redundant and meaningless (janma-vaiyarthyam), and production would never cease. 2. Origination from another (parataḥ): Held by Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika (Asatkāryavāda); refuted because if an effect could be produced from an entirely other/alien cause, then thick darkness could be produced from a blazing lamp. 3. Origination from both (dvābhyām): Held by Jaina metaphysics (Anekāntavāda); refuted because it merely combines the absurdities and contradictions of both self-origination and other-origination. 4. Uncaused origination (ahetutaḥ): Held by Cārvāka / Svabhāvavāda; refuted because if things were uncaused, effects would arise anywhere, at any time, without restriction, or never arise at all.**

#### Philosophical Grounding & Primary Source:
In Candrakīrti’s *Prasannapadā* on MMK 1.1:
1. **First Koṭi (*na svataḥ* - Refutation of Sāṁkhya)**: If the jar is identical with the clay (*tādātmya*), the clay is already the jar. The jar is already existent. Producing what is already existent is totally futile (*utpannasya punar utpādo niṣprayojanaḥ*). Moreover, if an existent entity is produced, it could never stop being produced.
2. **Second Koṭi (*na parataḥ* - Refutation of Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika and Buddhist Sarvāstivāda)**: If the effect is utterly different (*atyanta-bhinna*) from the cause, then any other entity could produce any other effect—*sarvaḥ sarvata utpadyeta* (flames could produce pitch darkness).
3. **Third Koṭi (*na dvābhyām* - Refutation of Jainism)**: Jainism holds that the effect is partly identical with and partly different from the cause (*bhedābheda*). Nāgārjuna points out that combining two flawed positions does not cure their defects; it simply doubles the logical faults (*ubhayapakṣa-bhaviṣyad-doṣa-prasādhyam*).
4. **Fourth Koṭi (*nāpy ahetutaḥ* - Refutation of Accidentalism/Svabhāvavāda/Cārvāka)**: If effects arose without cause, then all intentional human action (*kārya-kāraṇa-pravṛtti*) would become meaningless, and anything could arise out of nothing at any place and time (*ahetutve sarvaṁ sarvadā sambhavet*).
5. **Conclusion**: Since an entity cannot arise from itself, nor from another, nor from both, nor without a cause, production (*utpāda*) is an unconstructed conceptual fiction (*prapañca*). In ultimate truth (*paramārtha*), nothing is ever born (*Ajātivāda*).

#### Distractor Analysis:
- Options B, C, and D misattribute the four philosophical schools behind the *koṭis* (e.g., claiming Nyāya holds self-origination or Buddhism holds uncaused origination), displaying complete confusion regarding the classic Indian doxographical mapping of MMK 1.1.

### Question 6 (Format A: Conceptual MCQ - Jaina Saptabhaṅgī-Naya: Structure of the Fourth Predicate)

In Jaina epistemology and dialectic (*Syādvāda* / *Saptabhaṅgī-naya*, as expounded in Malliṣeṇa’s *Syādvādamañjarī* and Vidyānandi’s *Aṣṭasāhasrī*), reality is evaluated through seven conditional predication statements.

Consider the transition from the third predicate to the fourth predicate:
- Predicate 3: *Syād asti ca nāsti ca* ("In a certain sense, it is, and in a certain sense, it is not").
- Predicate 4: *Syād avaktavyaḥ* ("In a certain sense, it is inexpressible / unutterable").

What is the precise conceptual necessity that compels Jaina logic to formulate **syād avaktavyaḥ** as an independent, fourth step, rather than treating it as redundant with the third predicate?

A) Because the third predicate represents the absolute dissolution of the object into emptiness, whereas the fourth predicate represents the subjective silence of the ascetic.  
B) Because the third predicate expresses existence and non-existence consecutively/in succession (*krama-arpaṇā*), whereas the fourth predicate attempts to express both existence and non-existence simultaneously/in a single unified act of expression (*yugapat-arpaṇā*), which exceeds the structural capacity of linguistic articulation (*avaktavya*).  
C) Because the fourth predicate belongs to the *Paryāyārthika-naya*, whereas the first three belong to the *Dravyārthika-naya*.  
D) Because the fourth predicate is introduced solely to appease the Advaitins by admitting that reality is ultimately illusory.

---

#### Correct Answer:
**B) Because the third predicate expresses existence and non-existence consecutively/in succession (krama-arpaṇā), whereas the fourth predicate attempts to express both existence and non-existence simultaneously/in a single unified act of expression (yugapat-arpaṇā), which exceeds the structural capacity of linguistic articulation (avaktavya).**

#### Philosophical Grounding & Primary Source:
In Malliṣeṇa’s *Syādvādamañjarī* (commentary on Hemacandra’s *Anyayogavyavacchedikā*, verse 23) and Guṇaratna’s *Tarkarahasyadīpikā*:
- The Jaina theory of predication distinguishes rigorously between **kramārpaṇā** (presentation in temporal sequence) and **yugapad-arpaṇā** (presentation simultaneously).
- In the **third limb** (*syād asti ca nāsti ca*), a substance is described as having being (*asti*) from the perspective of its own substance, place, time, and nature (*sva-dravya-kṣetra-kāla-bhāva*), and as having non-being (*nāsti*) from the perspective of alien substance, place, time, and nature (*para-dravya-kṣetra-kāla-bhāva*). These two predications are made **consecutively** (*krameṇa*): first *asti*, then *nāsti*.
- But what happens if one attempts to express both *asti* (affirmation) and *nāsti* (negation) **simultaneously in a single, unitary lexical word** (*yugapat ekasmin pade* / *abheda-vṛttyā*)?
- Language is inherently discursive and sequential; no single composite word can simultaneously assert and negate an identical subject without freezing verbal expression.
- Because reality itself possesses this simultaneous dual nature, but human language lacks a single unitary vocable to utter it in one breath, the object is designated as **inexpressible** (*avaktavya*). *Avaktavya* does not mean the object is absolutely unknowable or non-existent (as in Advaita or Mādhyamika); it means it cannot be captured by a single, simultaneous positive-negative verbal signifier. This creates the unique fourth limb, generating limbs 5, 6, and 7 through permutations with the first three.

#### Distractor Analysis:
- **Option A is incorrect**: Jainism never admits absolute dissolution into emptiness; reality is indestructible (*dhruva*).
- **Option C is incorrect**: Both *Dravyārthika* (substantive) and *Paryāyārthika* (modal) nayas interpenetrate all seven steps; it is the mode of presentation (*kramārpaṇā* vs. *yugapad-arpaṇā*) that defines the fourth step.
- **Option D is incorrect**: The Jaina *avaktavya* is vehemently distinguished from the Advaitic *anirvacanīya*. In Advaita, *anirvacanīya* implies falsity (*mithyātva*); in Jainism, *avaktavya* expresses the rich, multifaceted, inexhaustible positive reality of the object.

### Question 7 (Format C: Multi-Statement MSQ - The Jaina Taxonomy of Nayavāda)

In Jaina epistemology (*Tattvārthasūtra* 1.33 of Umāsvāti and *Nyāyāvatāra* of Siddhasena Divākara), **Nayavāda** (the doctrine of partial viewpoints) systematizes cognitive perspectives into seven distinct *nayas*.

Evaluate the following statements regarding the seven *nayas*:

I. The *Dravyārthika-nayas* (substantial standpoints) comprise *Naigama* (teleological/common-sense), *Saṁgraha* (universalizing/class), and *Vyavahāra* (empirical/practical classification).  
II. The *Paryāyārthika-nayas* (modal standpoints) prioritize the changing, temporal, and differentiated aspects of reality, beginning with *Ṛjusūtra-naya*, which restricts reality solely to the present instantaneous moment.  
III. *Śabda-naya* accepts that synonymous words (like *Indra*, *Śakra*, and *Purandara*) denote entirely different ontological objects based on their distinct etymological roots.  
IV. *Evambhūta-naya* is the most restrictive of all nayas, asserting that a name can only be truthfully applied to an object when the object is actively performing the exact functional activity signified by that name (e.g., calling someone *pūjaka* only while they are in the active physical act of worship).

Which of the statements given above are strictly **CORRECT**?

A) I, II, and IV only  
B) I and III only  
C) II, III, and IV only  
D) I, II, III, and IV

---

#### Correct Answer:
**A) I, II, and IV only**

#### Philosophical Grounding & Primary Source:
In Umāsvāti’s *Tattvārthasūtra* (1.33: *naigama-saṁgraha-vyavahāra-ṛjusūtra-śabda-samabhirūḍha-evambhūtā nayāḥ*) and Pūjyapāda’s *Sarvārthasiddhi*:
- **Statement I is correct**: The first three nayas are *Dravyārthika* because they apprehend the underlying, enduring substance (*dravya*). *Naigama* considers purpose/end-result; *Saṁgraha* groups particulars under general universals; *Vyavahāra* divides the universals into pragmatic sub-classes.
- **Statement II is correct**: The remaining four are *Paryāyārthika* because they attend to modes/states (*paryāya*). *Ṛjusūtra* (the "straight-thread" view) cuts off past and future, apprehending only the currently existing, present-moment reality (very close to the Buddhist view).
- **Statement III is INCORRECT**: Statement III conflates *Śabda-naya* with *Samabhirūḍha-naya*. 
  - *Śabda-naya* (the verbal standpoint) recognizes grammatical correctness, tense, gender, and case, but **accepts synonyms** as referring to the same object (e.g., *Indra*, *Śakra*, and *Purandara* refer to the same king of gods).
  - It is **Samabhirūḍha-naya** (the etymological/conventional standpoint) that rejects synonymy, asserting that because their etymological derivations differ (*Indra* = all-powerful, *Śakra* = capable, *Purandara* = destroyer of cities), each word denotes a distinct nuance or phase of the object.
- **Statement IV is correct**: *Evambhūta-naya* ("the 'such-actuated' standpoint") is the ultimate, most rigorous linguistic specialization. It insists that an object can only bear a title when the activity denoted by that title is actively occurring. One is a "cook" (*pācaka*) only while actively stirring the pot, not while asleep.

#### Distractor Analysis:
- Since Statement III incorrectly assigns the rejection of synonyms to *Śabda-naya* rather than *Samabhirūḍha-naya*, any option containing III (B, C, and D) is invalid.

### Question 8 (Format B: Assertion-Reason - Cārvāka Critique of Vyāpti)

Given below are two statements: one is labelled as **Assertion (A)** and the other is labelled as **Reason (R)**:

**Assertion (A)**: In the *Tattvopaplavasiṁha* of Jayarāśi Bhaṭṭa and the Cārvāka chapter of the *Sarvadarśanasaṁgraha*, the Cārvāka radically rejects the validity of inference (*anumāna*) by demonstrating the impossibility of establishing *vyāpti* (invariable concomitance).  
**Reason (R)**: *Vyāpti* cannot be established through perception because perception is confined to present, proximate particulars and cannot grasp all past, distant, and future cases; nor can it be established through inference, because that would require another *vyāpti*, precipitating an infinite regress (*anavasthā*); nor through testimony, because testimony itself relies on inference.

In light of the above statements, choose the correct option:
A) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).  
B) Both (A) and (R) are true, but (R) is NOT the correct explanation of (A).  
C) (A) is true, but (R) is false.  
D) (A) is false, but (R) is true.

---

#### Correct Answer:
**A) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).**

#### Philosophical Grounding & Primary Source:
In Mādhavācārya’s *Sarvadarśanasaṁgraha* (Chapter 1: *Cārvākadarśanam*) and Jayarāśi Bhaṭṭa’s *Tattvopaplavasiṁha*:
- The Cārvāka epistemologist targets the core foundation of Indian logic: *vyāpti* (the universal, unconditional relation between *hetu* and *sādhya*, e.g., wherever there is smoke, there is fire).
- How is *vyāpti* known?
  1. **Not by External Perception (*bāhya-pratyakṣa*)**: Sensory organs can only come into contact (*sannikarṣa*) with particular objects existing here and now. They cannot perceive all past, future, and unperceived smokes and fires.
  2. **Not by Internal Perception (*mānasa-pratyakṣa*)**: The mind (*manas*) cannot observe external relations without the aid of external senses.
  3. **Not by Sāmānyalakṣaṇa-sannikarṣa**: Postulating an extraordinary perception of all particulars via universals is a groundless metaphysical invention; perceiving the universal "smokeness" does not prove that every future smoke will be accompanied by fire.
  4. **Not by Inference (*anumāna*)**: To prove *vyāpti* via inference requires a second inference, which requires a second *vyāpti*, leading to vicious infinite regress (*anavasthā-pāta*).
  5. **Not by Verbal Testimony (*śabda*)**: Words are either based on perception (which fails to grasp universality) or inference (which is in question). Moreover, trusting words requires an inference that the speaker is trustworthy (*āpta*).
  6. **Not by Absence of Upādhi (*anupādhikatva*)**: To know that a relation is free of conditions (*upādhi*), one must know all possible conditions in the universe across all time, which requires omniscience.
- Therefore, *vyāpti* is an unproven assumption, and inference is merely a practical guess (*saṁbhāvanā*) without apodictic epistemic validity (*prāmāṇya*). Thus, (R) provides the exact, exhaustive philosophical argument for (A).

#### Distractor Analysis:
- **Option B is incorrect**: (R) is the precise dialectical architecture used by Cārvāka to establish (A).
- **Options C and D are incorrect**: Both statements accurately reproduce the classical critique of induction in Indian philosophy.

### Question 9 (Format A: Conceptual MCQ - Purandara's Nuance on Cārvāka Epistemology)

In advanced classical Indian doxographies (such as Kamalaśīla’s *Tattvasaṁgrahapañjikā* and Vācaspati Miśra’s *Bhāmatī*), a critical distinction is drawn between radical Cārvāka skeptics (*Dhūrta Cārvāka* / Jayarāśi) and sophisticated Cārvāka logicians represented by **Purandara** (*Susikṣita Cārvāka*).

What is **Purandara’s** specific, refined position regarding the epistemic legitimacy of inference (*anumāna*)?

A) Purandara accepts inference regarding both transcendental entities (like God, heaven, and soul) and empirical entities, provided the inference is confirmed by the Vedas.  
B) Purandara accepts *lokaprasiddha-anumāna* (inferences concerning observable, empirical matters of everyday life, such as inferring fire from smoke), because they are verifiable by direct perception, but strictly rejects *alaukika- / paraloka-sādhaka-anumāna* (inferences that posit unobservable, transcendent entities like Īśvara, Adṛṣṭa, Atman, or the afterlife), because such transcendent inferences transcend the horizon of empirical verification.  
C) Purandara rejects all empirical inferences but accepts mathematical inferences.  
D) Purandara holds that inference is valid only when conducted by a king or state authority.

---

#### Correct Answer:
**B) Purandara accepts lokaprasiddha-anumāna (inferences concerning observable, empirical matters of everyday life, such as inferring fire from smoke), because they are verifiable by direct perception, but strictly rejects alaukika- / paraloka-sādhaka-anumāna (inferences that posit unobservable, transcendent entities like Īśvara, Adṛṣṭa, Atman, or the afterlife), because such transcendent inferences transcend the horizon of empirical verification.**

#### Philosophical Grounding & Primary Source:
In Kamalaśīla’s *Tattvasaṁgrahapañjikā* (commentary on verse 1482):
$$\text{"purandaras tv āha—loka-prasiddham anumānaṁ cārvākair apīṣyata eva,}$$
$$\text{yat tu kaiścid laukikam mārga-matītya anumānam ucyate, tan niṣidhyate."}$$
("Purandara, however, says: Inferences well-known in ordinary worldly life are indeed accepted even by the Cārvākas; but that inference which is declared by certain philosophers by transcending the worldly empirical realm—that alone is rejected.")

Purandara recognized that a total, dogmatic rejection of all inference would render everyday human life impossible: one could not even cook food (inferring that fire cooks), walk on a street (inferring the ground will hold), or communicate. 
Therefore, Purandara formulated a crucial epistemological demarcation:
1. **Loka-prasiddha / Utpannapratīti Anumāna**: Inferences whose conclusions can, in principle, be directly confirmed by subsequent perception (e.g., inferring fire from smoke on a nearby hill and then walking up to see the fire). This practical, empirically bounded inference is admitted.
2. **Atīndriya / Paraloka Anumāna**: Inferences constructed by theologians and metaphysicians to establish unobservable entities that can *never* be checked or confirmed by perception (such as an unembodied Soul, Karma/Adṛṣṭa, Heaven, Hell, or an eternal Creator God). This metaphysical leap into the transcendental void is categorically rejected as illegitimate and pseudo-logical.

#### Distractor Analysis:
- **Option A is incorrect**: Cārvāka never accepts the authority of the Vedas or transcendent entities; Purandara's whole goal was to dismantle transcendent inferences while salvaging worldly common-sense inference.
- **Option C is incorrect**: Classical Indian materialists did not bifurcate logic into empirical vs. pure mathematical formalism in the modern Cartesian sense.
- **Option D is incorrect**: This trivializes Purandara’s serious epistemological distinction between empirical and transcendent domains into arbitrary political decree.

### Question 10 (Format D: Comparative Matching - The Twelve Nidānas of Pratītyasamutpāda)

In the Buddhist doctrine of Dependent Origination (*Pratītyasamutpāda* / *Dvādaśāṅga-bhavacakra*, as codified in the *Pratītyasamutpādasūtra* and Vasubandhu’s *Abhidharmakośa* III), the twelve links span three temporal lives (past, present, future).

Match the specific Nidānas in **Column I** with their precise psychological-existential definition in **Column II**, and their temporal life allocation in **Column III**:

| Column I (Nidāna) | Column II (Existential/Psychological Function) | Column III (Temporal Life) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **P. Saṁskāra** | 1. The six cognitive-sensory domains (five physical senses + mind-base) emerging in the embryo. | i. Past Life (*Atīta-janma*) |
| **Q. Nāmarūpa** | 2. Volitional karmic formations (bodily, verbal, mental) conditioned by fundamental ignorance. | ii. Present Life (*Pratyutpanna-janma*) |
| **R. Ṣaḍāyatana** | 3. The psychosomatic organism (mental factors + physical embryonic matrix) taking shape in the womb. | iii. Future Life (*Anāgata-janma*) |
| **S. Jāti** | 4. Re-birth / the emergence of the five aggregates anew in a future existence. | |

Choose the correct matching code:
A) P-2-i, Q-3-ii, R-1-ii, S-4-iii  
B) P-1-i, Q-2-ii, R-3-ii, S-4-iii  
C) P-2-ii, Q-3-i, R-1-iii, S-4-iii  
D) P-3-i, Q-1-ii, R-2-ii, S-4-iii  

---

#### Correct Answer:
**A) P-2-i, Q-3-ii, R-1-ii, S-4-iii**

#### Philosophical Grounding & Primary Source:
In Vasubandhu’s *Abhidharmakośa* (Chapter III, verses 20–25):
The 12 Nidānas are divided across three lives:
1. **Past Life (2 links)**:
   - *Avidyā* (Ignorance)
   - *Saṁskāra* (Volitional karmic formations) -> **P-2-i**
2. **Present Life (8 links)**:
   - *Vijñāna* (Relinking consciousness entering the womb)
   - *Nāmarūpa* (The psycho-physical embryo) -> **Q-3-ii**
   - *Ṣaḍāyatana* (The six sense bases developing in the fetus) -> **R-1-ii**
   - *Sparśa* (Contact between sense, object, and consciousness)
   - *Vedanā* (Feeling tone: pleasant, unpleasant, neutral)
   - *Tṛṣṇā* (Craving/thirst)
   - *Upādāna* (Clinging/grasping)
   - *Bhava* (Karmic becoming/active potency for new existence)
3. **Future Life (2 links)**:
   - *Jāti* (Rebirth in a future life) -> **S-4-iii**
   - *Jarā-maraṇa* (Old age, lamentation, and death)

#### Distractor Analysis:
- Any variation from **A (P-2-i, Q-3-ii, R-1-ii, S-4-iii)** disrupts the chronological sequence and structural tripartite division of the *Bhavacakra*. For example, placing *Saṁskāra* in the present life or *Ṣaḍāyatana* in the future life violates the standard Abhidharma temporal taxonomy.
