### Question 1 (Format A: Conceptual MCQ - Navya-Nyāya Definition of Vyāpti)

In the *Vyāpti-pañcaka* section of the *Anumānakhaṇḍa* of Gaṅgeśa Upādhyāya’s *Tattvacintāmaṇi*, five preliminary definitions of *vyāpti* (invariable concomitance) are formulated and successively rejected. Consider the first tentative definition:
$$\text{Vyāpti} = \text{sādhyābhāvavad-avṛttitvam} \quad (\text{non-occurrence of the probans in the loci of the absence of the probandum}).$$

Why does Gaṅgeśa reject this definition as suffering from the defect of *avyāpti* (under-extension / failure to cover legitimate inferences)?

A) Because in valid *kevalānvayi* inferences such as *"This is nameable because it is knowable"* (*idaṁ vācyaṁ prameyatvāt*), the probandum (*vācyatva*) is universally present (*sarvavṛtti*), rendering *sādhyābhāva* (the absence of the probandum) strictly unexemplified (*aprasiddha*), so the locus of its absence cannot be identified.  
B) Because in standard *anvaya-vyatireki* inferences such as *"The mountain has fire because it has smoke"*, the absence of fire exists in the lake, but smoke also exists in the lake through the relation of inherence (*samavāya*), causing over-extension (*ativyāpti*).  
C) Because the definition fails to incorporate *avacchedaka* (limitor) technology, thereby allowing a counter-instance where fire exists in a kitchen as kitchen-fire (*mahānasīya-vahni*), rendering the absence of hill-fire present even on the smoky mountain.  
D) Because the relation determining locus-hood (*vṛttitā-niyāmaka-sambandha*) is not specified as contact (*saṁyoga*), thereby confusing mental absence (*buddhīya-abhāva*) with ontological absence (*vastu-svarūpa-abhāva*).

---

#### Correct Answer:
**A) Because in valid kevalānvayi inferences such as "This is nameable because it is knowable" (idaṁ vācyaṁ prameyatvāt), the probandum (vācyatva) is universally present (sarvavṛtti), rendering sādhyābhāva (the absence of the probandum) strictly unexemplified (aprasiddha), so the locus of its absence cannot be identified.**

#### Philosophical Grounding & Primary Source:
In Gaṅgeśa’s *Tattvacintāmaṇi* (Anumānakhaṇḍa, *Vyāptivāda*), the first definition evaluated is *sādhyābhāvavad-avṛttitvam* (the non-existence of the *hetu* in that which possesses the absence of the *sādhya*). Gaṅgeśa demonstrates that this definition breaks down fatally in the case of *kevalānvayi-anumāna* (inferences with a universally positive probandum). In an inference like *idaṁ vācyaṁ jñeyatvāt* or *idaṁ vācyaṁ prameyatvāt*, every knowable or existent entity in the Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika ontology is nameable (*vācya*). Consequently, there is no entity anywhere in the cosmos where the absence of nameability (*vācyatvābhāva*) exists. The absence of the *sādhya* is entirely unexemplified (*aprasiddha* / *alīka*). If *sādhyābhāva* does not exist, then *sādhyābhāvavat* (the locus possessing such absence) is a null set, and one cannot determine the non-occurrence (*avṛttitvam*) of the *hetu* therein. Thus, a definition that requires locating *sādhyābhāva* fails to cover valid *kevalānvayi* inferences, suffering from the cardinal logical fault of *avyāpti*. This necessitates Gaṅgeśa's eventual formulation of the *Siddhānta-lakṣaṇa*: *pratiyogyanadhikaraṇa-kāpīkṣaṇāvacchinna-sādhyasamānādhikaraṇa...* / *hetusamānādhikaraṇa-virahāpratiyogitā-avacchedakāvacchinna-sādhyasāmānādhikaraṇyaṁ vyāptiḥ*.

#### Distractor Analysis:
- **Option B is incorrect**: In Nyāya, the relation by which the *hetu* occurs is implicitly or explicitly governed by the relation of occurrence (*hetutāvacchedaka-sambandha*). Smoke does not occur in a lake by inherence in any standard counter-argument; rather, the standard issue with inherence is that smoke inheres only in its parts (smoke-particles), not in the lake or the mountain. Moreover, B misidentifies the defect as *ativyāpti* rather than the foundational *avyāpti* regarding *kevalānvayi*.
- **Option C is incorrect**: While the problem of distinguishing generic absence (*sāmānyābhāva*) from specific absence (*viśeṣābhāva*, such as *mahānasīyavahnyabhāva*) is a central preoccupation in Navya-Nyāya, it is not the reason Gaṅgeśa rejects the very first formulation in the *Vyāpti-pañcaka*. That specific challenge is treated under *vyadhikaraṇa-dharmāvacchinnābhāva* and the qualification of *sādhyatāvacchedaka-sambandha*.
- **Option D is incorrect**: Nyāya rigorously rejects purely "mental absences" (*buddhīya-abhāva*) in its realist epistemology; absences (*abhāva*) are objective ontological padārthas. This formulation uses pseudo-Navya terminology to mask an irrelevant metaphysical thesis.

### Question 2 (Format B: Assertion-Reason - Alaukika Sannikarṣa in Nyāya)

Given below are two statements: one is labelled as **Assertion (A)** and the other is labelled as **Reason (R)**:

**Assertion (A)**: In Nyāya epistemology, the perception of universal concomitance (*vyāptigraha*) between smoke-in-general and fire-in-general across all spatial and temporal instances is epistemically established through an extraordinary sensory connection (*alaukika-sannikarṣa*) designated as *sāmānyalakṣaṇa-pratyāsatti*.  
**Reason (R)**: If *sāmānyalakṣaṇa-sannikarṣa* were not admitted, the mind could only perceive smoke and fire in particular loci (*viśeṣa*) via ordinary sensory contact (*laukika-sannikarṣa*), leaving an unresolved doubt (*saṁśaya*) regarding unperceived instances, which would permanently paralyze the possibility of inductive generalization (*vyāpti-niścaya*).

In light of the above statements, choose the correct option:
A) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).  
B) Both (A) and (R) are true, but (R) is NOT the correct explanation of (A).  
C) (A) is true, but (R) is false.  
D) (A) is false, but (R) is true.

---

#### Correct Answer:
**A) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).**

#### Philosophical Grounding & Primary Source:
In Viśvanātha’s *Bhāṣāpariccheda* and *Siddhāntamuktāvalī* (Pratyakṣakhaṇḍa, verses 63–65), as well as Gaṅgeśa’s *Tattvacintāmaṇi*, *alaukika-pratyakṣa* is categorized into three types: *sāmānyalakṣaṇa*, *jñānalakṣaṇa*, and *yogaja*. The necessity of *sāmānyalakṣaṇa-pratyāsatti* arises precisely from the epistemological dilemma of induction. Ordinary perception (*laukika-sannikarṣa*) brings the sensory organ into contact with a finite set of spatiotemporally conditioned particulars (e.g., this kitchen-smoke and this kitchen-fire). However, *vyāpti* is an unconditioned relation between smoke-as-such (*dhūmatva-avacchinna*) and fire-as-such (*vahnitva-avacchinna*), spanning all past, present, distant, and future smokes. If our cognitive contact were restricted to proximate particulars, the mind would always be haunted by the skeptical doubt (*vyabhicāra-saṁśaya*): "Perhaps in some unperceived mountain or future time, smoke exists without fire." In *sāmānyalakṣaṇa-sannikarṣa*, the universal (*sāmānya*, e.g., *dhūmatva*), when perceived in a present particular via ordinary contact (*saṁyukta-samavāya*), itself acts as an extraordinary cognitive medium (*pratyāsatti*) through which all individuals possessing that universal (*tad-āśraya*) become presented to consciousness as objects of cognition. Hence, (R) provides the exact philosophical and systemic justification for (A).

#### Distractor Analysis:
- **Option B is incorrect**: (R) is not a disconnected truth; it is the definitive teleological and logical justification formulated by the Naiyāyika to defeat the Cārvāka skeptical critique of induction and to differentiate the Nyāya account of *vyāptigraha* from the Bhāṭṭa Mīmāṁsā view (which relies on repeated observation, *bhūyodarśana*, without positing *alaukika-sannikarṣa*).
- **Option C is incorrect**: (R) is directly true in Nyāya literature (cf. *Nyāyasūtra* 1.1.4 commentary and *Nyāyamañjarī* of Jayanta Bhaṭṭa).
- **Option D is incorrect**: (A) is an established, fundamental thesis of the Prācīna and Navya-Nyāya systems.

### Question 3 (Format C: Multi-Statement MSQ - Taxonomy and Demarcation of Hetvābhāsas)

According to the classical Nyāya system (*Nyāyasūtra* 1.2.4 and Annambhaṭṭa’s *Tarkasaṁgraha-Dīpikā*), five distinct fallacies of the probans (*hetvābhāsa*) are recognized: *savyabhicāra* (inconclusive), *viruddha* (contradictory), *satpratipakṣa* (inferentially counterbalanced), *asiddha* (unproven/unfounded), and *bādhita* (perceptually or epistemically sublated). 

Examine the following four statements regarding their strict boundary conditions and logical properties:

I. In *satpratipakṣa*, the inferential conclusion is obstructed by an equally potent counter-probans (*tulyabala-hetvantara*) proving the absence of the probandum, whereas in *bādhita*, the absence of the probandum is established not by an inference, but by an epistemically prior and stronger pramāṇa such as perception or reliable testimony.  
II. The fallacy of *anupasaṁhārin* (a subdivision of *savyabhicāra*) occurs when the probans lacks both affirmative (*sapakṣa*) and negative (*vipakṣa*) instances because the subject (*pakṣa*) is all-inclusive, as in *"Everything is non-eternal because it is knowable"*.  
III. *Āśrayāsiddha* occurs when the probans is absent from the subject due to an extrinsic conditional factor (*upādhi*), whereas *svarūpāsiddha* occurs when the subject itself is a fictitious or non-existent entity, such as a "sky-lotus".  
IV. A *viruddha-hetu* is one that is pervaded by the absence of the probandum (*sādhyābhāva-vyāpta*), meaning it invariably proves the contradictory of what it was advanced to prove (e.g., *"Sound is eternal, because it is produced"*).

Which of the statements given above are strictly **CORRECT**?

A) I, II, and IV only  
B) I, III, and IV only  
C) II, III, and IV only  
D) I, II, III, and IV

---

#### Correct Answer:
**A) I, II, and IV only**

#### Philosophical Grounding & Primary Source:
- **Statement I is correct**: *Nyāyasūtra* 1.2.6 defines *viruddha*, 1.2.7 defines *satpratipakṣa* (*prakaraṇasama*), and 1.2.9 defines *bādhita* (*kālātīta*). In *satpratipakṣa*, two rival inferences of equal epistemic strength clash, leading to cognitive suspension (*saṁśaya-janaka*). In *bādhita*, the absence of the *sādhya* is already determined by a non-inferential, stronger pramāṇa (*pramāṇāntareṇa niścita*), e.g., *"Fire is cold, because it is a substance"*—here, coldness is contradicted by direct tactile perception (*sparśana-pratyakṣa*).
- **Statement II is correct**: *Anupasaṁhārin* (the non-exclusive inconclusive) is characterized by the absence of any *dṛṣṭānta* (corroborating instance). In *sarvam anityam prameyatvāt*, everything is included under the *pakṣa* (*sarva*); hence, no distinct *sapakṣa* (where *sādhya* definitely exists) or *vipakṣa* (where *sādhya* definitely does not exist) remains available to instantiate *vyāpti* (*anvayavyatireka-dṛṣṭānta-rahitaḥ*).
- **Statement III is INCORRECT**: Statement III reverses the classical definitions of *āśrayāsiddha* and *svarūpāsiddha*. *Āśrayāsiddha* occurs when the *pakṣa* (the substrate/locus) itself is non-existent (*pakṣatāvacchedakābhāva*), such as *gaganāravindaṁ surabhi aravindatvāt* ("The sky-lotus is fragrant, because it is a lotus")—the sky-lotus does not exist as an *āśraya*. *Svarūpāsiddha* occurs when the *hetu* does not exist in the *pakṣa* (*pakṣe hetor abhāvaḥ*), e.g., *"Sound is a quality, because it is visible"*—visibility does not exist in sound. Extrinsic conditions (*upādhi*) pertain to *vyāpyatvāsiddha*, not *āśrayāsiddha*.
- **Statement IV is correct**: *Sādhyābhāva-vyāpto hetur viruddhaḥ* (*Tarkasaṁgraha*). A *viruddha* probans is that which is invariably accompanied by the negation of the intended *sādhya*. For example, *śabdo nityaḥ kṛtakatvāt* ("Sound is eternal, because it is produced")—product-hood (*kṛtakatva*) is pervaded by non-eternity (*anityatva*), which is the absolute contradiction of eternity.

#### Distractor Analysis:
- Since Statement III contains an overt conceptual inversion of *āśrayāsiddha* and *svarūpāsiddha*, any option containing III (B, C, and D) is definitively erroneous.

### Question 4 (Format A: Conceptual MCQ - Svataḥ vs. Parataḥ Prāmāṇyavāda)

The classical debate on the validity of cognition (*prāmāṇyavāda*) between Mīmāṁsā and Nyāya turns on two distinct moments: the origination of validity (*utpatti*) and the apprehension/ascertainment of validity (*jñapti*). 

Which of the following formulations precisely articulates the **Prābhākara Mīmāṁsā** position on *prāmāṇya*, and why does it reject the Nyāya doctrine of *parataḥ-prāmāṇya* as leading to *anavasthā* (infinite regress)?

A) Prābhākara holds that validity is produced by special excellences (*guṇa*) in the causal apparatus and known via *anuvyavasāya*, but rejects subsequent confirmation because all cognitions are intrinsically invalid until proven true.  
B) Prābhākara holds the doctrine of *triputī-pratyakṣavāda*, wherein every cognition simultaneously and self-luminously reveals the object (*viṣaya*), the knowing self (*jñātā*), and the cognition itself (*jñāna*); thus, all cognitions are intrinsically valid (*svataḥ-pramāṇa*) *qua* cognition, and "error" is merely non-apprehension (*akhyāti*). If validity required an extrinsic inference (*parataḥ*), that secondary inference would require a tertiary one to validate it, resulting in *anavasthā*.  
C) Prābhākara accepts *svataḥ-utpatti* but insists upon *parataḥ-jñapti*, arguing that validity arises from the absence of defect (*doṣābhāva*), but can only be known through practical success (*saṁvādi-pravṛtti*).  
D) Prābhākara aligns with Kumārila Bhaṭṭa in asserting that validity is apprehended by an inference based on the epistemic quality of "knownness" (*jñātatā*) produced in the object, rejecting the Nyāya view solely because Nyāya denies the mind-organ (*manas*) direct access to external relations.

---

#### Correct Answer:
**B) Prābhākara holds the doctrine of triputī-pratyakṣavāda, wherein every cognition simultaneously and self-luminously reveals the object (viṣaya), the knowing self (jñātā), and the cognition itself (jñāna); thus, all cognitions are intrinsically valid (svataḥ-pramāṇa) qua cognition, and "error" is merely non-apprehension (akhyāti). If validity required an extrinsic inference (parataḥ), that secondary inference would require a tertiary one to validate it, resulting in anavasthā.**

#### Philosophical Grounding & Primary Source:
In Śālikanātha Miśra’s *Prakaraṇapañcikā* (*Pramāṇapārāyaṇa* section) and Prabhākara’s *Bṛhatī*, the Prābhākara school upholds *svataḥ-prāmāṇyavāda* in both *utpatti* (origination) and *jñapti* (apprehension) to its ultimate radical conclusion: *sarvaṁ jñānaṁ dharmiṇi abhrāntam* ("All cognitions are valid regarding their intentional substrate"). Under Prabhākara’s *tripuṭī-saṁvit-vāda*, every act of awareness illuminates three components simultaneously: the subject (*ahaṁ-vitti*), the object (*viṣaya-vitti*), and the awareness itself (*sva-saṁvitti*). Cognition is self-luminous (*svaprakāśa*); it never errs in presenting its content. What common discourse calls "error" (e.g., mistaking mother-of-pearl for silver) is not a single erroneous cognition (*anyathākhyāti*), but a composite failure to discriminate (*bhedāgraha* / *akhyāti*) between a present perceptual presentation ("this") and a recalled memory-image ("silver"). 

The Mīmāṁsā critique against the Nyāya thesis (*parataḥ-prāmāṇyavāda*—that cognition is validated extrinsically by a subsequent inference based on successful action, *pravṛtti-sāmarthya-liṅgaka-anumāna*) is the threat of vicious infinite regress (*anavasthā*). If cognition $C_1$ requires cognition $C_2$ (the perception of fruitful activity) to validate it, and $C_2$ is itself a cognition that needs an extrinsic validating cognition $C_3$, then no cognition could ever attain certainty, leading to epistemic paralysis (*apratisṭhā*). Therefore, validity must be intrinsic (*svataḥ*), born of the natural causal conditions of cognition itself (*jñāna-sāmagrī-janya*), without depending on extrinsic excellences (*guṇa*).

#### Distractor Analysis:
- **Option A is incorrect**: Positing *guṇa* as the cause of validity and *anuvyavasāya* as the apprehending vehicle is the classical **Nyāya** position, precisely the view Prabhākara rejects.
- **Option C is incorrect**: This describes a hybrid stance (somewhat resembling the view of certain Buddhists or Murāri Miśra's nuances), but Prabhākara is uncompromisingly *svataḥ* in both *utpatti* and *jñapti*.
- **Option D is incorrect**: The doctrine of *jñātatā* (knownness / cognizedness of the object) as the mark from which cognition is inferred belongs exclusively to the **Bhāṭṭa Mīmāṁsā** school of Kumārila Bhaṭṭa (*Ślokavārttika*, *Pratyakṣasūtra*). Prabhākara emphatically rejects *jñātatā*, maintaining instead direct self-illumination (*tripuṭīpratyakṣa*).

### Question 5 (Format A: Conceptual MCQ - Arthāpatti: Mīmāṁsā vs. Nyāya Reducibility)

Kumārila Bhaṭṭa in the *Ślokavārttika* (*Arthāpatti-pariccheda*) defends *arthāpatti* (postulation / presumption) as an autonomous pramāṇa, whereas the Naiyāyikas (from Vātsyāyana to Gaṅgeśa) argue that it is fully reducible to an exclusively negative inference (*kevalavyatirekī anumāna*). 

What is Kumārila’s definitive ground for rejecting the Nyāya reduction of *arthāpatti* (e.g., *"Fat Devadatta does not eat by day, yet remains fat; therefore, he eats at night"*) to *anumāna*?

A) Because in *arthāpatti*, the probans (*hetu*) is completely non-existent, whereas every valid inference requires a positive *hetu* residing in the *pakṣa*.  
B) Because *anumāna* operates solely when the *pakṣa* is doubt-free, whereas *arthāpatti* operates when there is cognitive conflict (*virodha*) between two known facts (Devadatta's living/fatness and his not eating by day), which generates an inner crisis that can only be resolved by presuming an explanatory fact (night-eating), without requiring prior knowledge of invariable concomitance (*vyāptigraha*).  
C) Because *arthāpatti* yields only probable belief (*saṁśaya*), whereas *anumāna* yields apodictic certainty (*pramā*).  
D) Because *vyatireki-vyāpti* is rejected by Kumārila Bhaṭṭa as an illegitimate form of universal concomitance, since Kumārila acknowledges only *anvaya-vyāpti*.

---

#### Correct Answer:
**B) Because anumāna operates solely when the pakṣa is doubt-free, whereas arthāpatti operates when there is cognitive conflict (virodha) between two known facts (Devadatta's living/fatness and his not eating by day), which generates an inner crisis that can only be resolved by presuming an explanatory fact (night-eating), without requiring prior knowledge of invariable concomitance (vyāptigraha).**

#### Philosophical Grounding & Primary Source:
In the *Ślokavārttika* (*Arthāpattipariccheda*, verses 1–10 and 30–45), Kumārila defines *arthāpatti* as: *dṛṣṭaḥ śruto vā'py artho 'nyathā na upapadyate iti adṛṣṭārtha-kalpanam* ("The postulation of an unseen fact without which an observed or scripturally heard fact cannot be intelligibly explained"). 
The Naiyāyika attempts to subsume this under inference via a *kevalavyatirekī* syllogism:
- *Pakṣa*: Fat Devadatta who does not eat by day.
- *Sādhya*: Eats at night (*rātri-bhojī*).
- *Hetu*: Being fat while not eating by day (*divā-abhuñjānatve sati pīnatvāt*).
- *Vyāpti* (Vyatireka): Whoever does not eat at night, while not eating by day, is not fat (like a fasting ascetic).

Kumārila and his commentators (such as Pārthasārathi Miśra in *Śāstradīpikā*) refute this reduction on crucial psychological and epistemic grounds:
1. In inference, the knowledge of *vyāpti* between the *hetu* and *sādhya* is the indispensable instrumental cause (*karaṇa*), operating through a deliberate consideration of the mark (*parāmarśa*). In *arthāpatti*, an individual immediately recognizes an apparent contradiction or incomprehensibility (*anupapatti* / *virodha*) between two established cognitions (Devadatta's stoutness and his complete daytime abstinence). The postulation of night-eating leaps into awareness directly to resolve this *virodha*.
2. There is no prior, ready-made grasp of *vyāpti* between "fatness without day-eating" and "night-eating" stored in memory; the postulation itself posits the fact to reconcile the conflict. The epistemic phenomenological texture (*pratīti-vilakṣaṇatva*) of *arthāpatti* is: "I am postulating an explanatory hypothesis to save a contradiction," not: "I am inferring a conclusion from a perceived mark grounded on a previously known universal invariable relation."

#### Distractor Analysis:
- **Option A is incorrect**: The probans in the Nyāya formulation is not "non-existent"; it consists of the combined property of stoutness conjoined with daytime abstinence, which is directly observed.
- **Option C is incorrect**: *Arthāpatti* in Mīmāṁsā is an authentic, incontrovertible pramāṇa producing *pramā* (valid knowledge), not mere conjecture, doubt (*saṁśaya*), or hypothesis (*tarka*).
- **Option D is incorrect**: Kumārila does not discard negative instances in induction wholesale; he explicitly utilizes *anvaya-vyatireka* in analyzing linguistic potency (*śakti*) and causal relations. His rejection of the Nyāya reduction of *arthāpatti* rests on the unique cognitive structure of *anupapatti* (inexplicability) versus *parāmarśa* (subsumptive reflection).

### Question 6 (Format B: Assertion-Reason - Anupalabdhi and the Epistemic Status of Abhava)

Given below are two statements: one is labelled as **Assertion (A)** and the other is labelled as **Reason (R)**:

**Assertion (A)**: Bhāṭṭa Mīmāṁsā and Advaita Vedānta postulate an autonomous sixth pramāṇa—*anupalabdhi* (non-apprehension)—to cognize the ontological category of absence (*abhāva*), explicitly rejecting the Nyāya thesis that *abhāva* is apprehended directly by sensory perception (*pratyakṣa*).  
**Reason (R)**: The sense-organs can only apprehend objects with which they can establish a legitimate physical contact (*sannikarṣa*), but between a physical sense-organ (like the eye) and an absence (which is non-substantial and qualityless), neither contact (*saṁyoga*) nor inherence (*samavāya*) is metaphysically possible; hence, the Nyāya relation of *viśeṣaṇa-viśeṣyabhāva* (qualifier-qualified relation) is an ad hoc pseudo-relation.

In light of the above statements, choose the correct option:
A) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).  
B) Both (A) and (R) are true, but (R) is NOT the correct explanation of (A).  
C) (A) is true, but (R) is false.  
D) (A) is false, but (R) is true.

---

#### Correct Answer:
**A) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).**

#### Philosophical Grounding & Primary Source:
In Kumārila Bhaṭṭa’s *Ślokavārttika* (*Abhāvapariccheda*) and Dharmarāja Adhvarin’s *Vedāntaparibhāṣā* (Chapter 6: *Anupalabdhi-pariccheda*), the epistemic necessity of *anupalabdhi* is rigorously established upon ontological principles of sensory contact. 

Nyāya claims that absence is perceived through the eye via the sixth *laukika-sannikarṣa*: *viśeṣaṇatā* / *viśeṣaṇa-viśeṣya-bhāva* (e.g., the absence of a pot on the floor is perceived because the absence is a qualifying attribute, *viśeṣaṇa*, of the floor, which is in direct contact, *saṁyoga*, with the eye: *saṁyukta-viśeṣaṇatā*).

Kumārila and the Advaitins launch a devastating critique against this Nyāya move:
1. *Sannikarṣa* must be a genuine relation (*sambandha*). In classical Indian realism, a relation requires two existent relata (*dvisṭha*). But *abhāva* is the negation of being.
2. The standard real relations are *saṁyoga* (contact, which occurs only between two *dravyas*) and *samavāya* (inherence, which occurs between inseparable entities like substance and quality/universal). Absence is neither a *dravya* (ruling out *saṁyoga*) nor does it inhere in the floor (ruling out *samavāya*).
3. The Naiyāyika's *viśeṣaṇa-viśeṣyabhāva* is not an independent relation, but merely an epistemic phrasing of a relation that must already rest on an underlying ontic link. One can say "the floor has absence" only if there is an antecedent relation connecting them. Since no physical connection exists between the eye and the non-existent jar, the eye cannot visually register the absence.
4. Therefore, it is the appropriate non-perception (*yogyānupalabdhi*)—the non-cognition of a perceptible object under conditions where all other factors for perception are present—that operates as an independent, negative epistemic instrument (*pramāṇa*) to grasp absence. Thus, (R) directly explains the exact metaphysical foundation of (A).

#### Distractor Analysis:
- **Option B is incorrect**: (R) is the precise, historically documented argument advanced by Kumārila against Vātsyāyana and Uddyotakara; it directly substantiates the assertion.
- **Option C is incorrect**: (R) is completely true according to Bhāṭṭa and Advaitic primary texts.
- **Option D is incorrect**: (A) is a canonical epistemological stance separating Bhāṭṭa Mīmāṁsā/Advaita from Nyāya, Prabhākara, and Buddhism.

### Question 7 (Format C: Multi-Statement MSQ - Upamāna Across Philosophical Systems)

The pramāṇa of *upamāna* (analogy / comparison) occupies radically divergent structural and epistemic roles in Nyāya, Mīmāṁsā, and Advaita Vedānta. 

Evaluate the veracity of the following four statements regarding the definition and scope of *upamāna*:

I. In Nyāya (*Nyāyasūtra* 1.1.6 and *Tarkasaṁgraha*), *upamāna* is defined as the instrument of the valid cognition of the relation between a name and the object denoted by it (*saṁjñā-saṁjñi-sambandha-jñāna*), sparked by an authoritative forester’s statement (*atideśa-vākya*) such as *"A wild cow (gavaya) resembles a domestic cow"*.  
II. In Bhāṭṭa Mīmāṁsā and Advaita Vedānta, the result of *upamāna* is NOT the naming relation of the *gavaya*, but rather the valid cognition of the similarity to the *gavaya* existing in the remembered domestic cow (*smaryamāṇāyāṁ gavi gavaya-sādṛśya-jñānam*).  
III. The Prābhākara Mīmāṁsā school rejects *upamāna* as an independent pramāṇa, reducing it entirely to *pratyakṣa* and *anumāna*, because it holds that similarity (*sādṛśya*) is not an independent ontological category.  
IV. In Advaita Vedānta, the perception of similarity in the *gavaya* serves as the auxiliary cause (*karaṇa*), while the remembered cow qualified by similarity to the *gavaya* is the content of the resultant pramā.

Which of the statements given above are strictly **CORRECT**?

A) I, II, and IV only  
B) I and III only  
C) II, III, and IV only  
D) I, II, III, and IV

---

#### Correct Answer:
**A) I, II, and IV only**

#### Philosophical Grounding & Primary Source:
- **Statement I is correct**: *Nyāyasūtra* 1.1.6: *prasiddhasādharmyāt sādhyasādhanam upamānam*. Annambhaṭṭa confirms: *upamitikaraṇam upamānam; saṁjñā-saṁjñi-sambandha-jñānam upamitiḥ; tatkaraṇaṁ sādṛśyajñānam*. A person learns from an expert (*atideśa-vākya*) that a *gavaya* is like a cow. When he goes to the forest, perceives an animal resembling a cow, recalls the assertion, and realizes: "This animal is what is denoted by the word *gavaya*", this semantic denotative knowledge is the unique fruit of *upamāna*.
- **Statement II is correct**: In *Ślokavārttika* (*Upamānapariccheda*, verses 3–12) and *Vedāntaparibhāṣā* (Chapter 5), Kumārila and Dharmarāja Adhvarin sharply critique the Nyāya view. They argue: The fact that this animal is named *gavaya* is known simply through verbal testimony (*śabda*) combined with direct perception (*pratyakṣa*). The authentic, non-redundant work of *upamāna* begins when a man in the forest, seeing the *gavaya*, has a cognition directed toward his domestic cow at home: *"My cow at home is similar to this forest gavaya"*. Because the domestic cow is absent and not currently connected to the eye, this similarity residing in the cow at home cannot be perceived by the senses, nor can it be inferred (for want of a *liṅga*). Hence, *upamāna* is the distinct pramāṇa that reveals similarity in an absent object.
- **Statement III is INCORRECT**: Prabhākara Mīmāṁsā does **NOT** reject *upamāna*. The Prābhākara school explicitly accepts five pramāṇas (*pratyakṣa, anumāna, śabda, upamāna, arthāpatti*), and maintains *upamāna* as an autonomous pramāṇa along lines very similar to Kumārila, defining *sādṛśya* as an objective category known through *upamāna*. It is the **Vaiśeṣika**, **Sāṁkhya**, and **Buddhist** traditions that reject *upamāna* as an independent pramāṇa, subsuming it under *anumāna* or *pratyakṣa*.
- **Statement IV is correct**: *Vedāntaparibhāṣā* specifies: *tatra gavayanirūpita-sādṛśyaviśiṣṭa-goniṣṭha-sādṛśyajñānaṁ karaṇam... gavayapratiyogika-sādṛśyaviśiṣṭatayā go-smaraṇaṁ vyāpāraḥ*. The perceptual cognition of similarity to a cow in the *gavaya* acts as the instrumental cause, leading to the resultant knowledge of the cow qualified by similarity to the *gavaya*.

#### Distractor Analysis:
- Since Statement III makes a demonstrably false claim regarding Prābhākara epistemology (Prabhākara famously accepts 5 pramāṇas, rejecting only *anupalabdhi*), any option containing III (B, C, and D) is definitively eliminated.

### Question 8 (Format A: Conceptual MCQ - Linguistic Meaning: Abhihitānvayavāda vs. Anvitābhidhānavāda)

In the philosophy of language (*Śabda-pramāṇa*), Kumārila Bhaṭṭa’s *Abhihitānvayavāda* and Prabhākara Miśra’s *Anvitābhidhānavāda* present opposing accounts of how sentence-meaning (*vākyārtha*) is generated from individual word-meanings (*padārtha*). 

Which of the following formulations correctly diagnoses the core technical divergence between the two schools regarding the expressive power of words (*śakti*)?

A) Kumārila holds that words possess *śakti* only to convey their isolated, uncombined universal meanings (*padārtha*), and the synthetic sentence-meaning (*anvaya*) is subsequently communicated through secondary implication (*lakṣaṇā*) or through the medium of the retrieved meanings themselves; whereas Prabhākara holds that words have *śakti* to denote meanings only as already syntactically and semantically related to an action (*kāryānvita-svārtha*).  
B) Kumārila asserts that sentence-meaning is perceived directly through auditory sensation, whereas Prabhākara asserts that sentence-meaning is an inferential construct derived from the speaker’s intention (*tātparya*).  
C) Kumārila accepts *sphoṭa* as the indivisible semantic burst transcending individual phonemes, whereas Prabhākara rejects *sphoṭa* in favor of atomic phonemic denotation (*varṇa-vāda*).  
D) Kumārila restricts the application of *śakti* entirely to verbs, whereas Prabhākara insists that nouns alone have primary denotative capacity, with verbs functioning merely as syntactic auxiliaries (*sahakāri*).

---

#### Correct Answer:
**A) Kumārila holds that words possess śakti only to convey their isolated, uncombined universal meanings (padārtha), and the synthetic sentence-meaning (anvaya) is subsequently communicated through secondary implication (lakṣaṇā) or through the medium of the retrieved meanings themselves; whereas Prabhākara holds that words have śakti to denote meanings only as already syntactically and semantically related to an action (kāryānvita-svārtha).**

#### Philosophical Grounding & Primary Source:
In Mīmāṁsā linguistic theory (*Ślokavārttika*, *Vākyādhikaraṇa*, and *Prakaraṇapañcikā*, *Vākyārthamātṛkā*):
1. **Kumārila’s Abhihitānvayavāda** ("Connection of the designated"): Words possess the denotative potency (*abhidhā-śakti*) only to bring to mind their respective, isolated lexical meanings—universals (*jāti*) unattached to any specific relational nexus. Once these word-meanings are presented (*abhihita*), they assemble themselves into a unified propositional meaning (*vākyārtha*) through the mutual operation of three indispensable syntactic-semantic auxiliaries: expectancy (*ākāṅkṣā*), semantic fitness (*yogyatā*), and spatio-temporal contiguity (*sannidhi*). The resulting synthetic relation (*anvaya*) is brought about by the *padārthas* acting through secondary signification (*lakṣaṇā* or a tertiary process).
2. **Prabhākara’s Anvitābhidhānavāda** ("Denotation of the connected"): Prabhākara rejects the idea that a word ever denotes a detached, isolated abstraction. Language is learned by the child observing elders' linguistic behavior (*vṛddha-vyavahāra*), which always takes the form of action-oriented commands (*kārya* / *niyoga*, e.g., *"Bring the cow"*). Words are never learned in isolation. Therefore, a word’s innate *śakti* (denotative capacity) is fundamentally characterized as *anvitābhidhāna*—denoting its own meaning as already connected (*anvita*) with the other factors of an action (*kriyā* / *kārya*). No secondary or post-lexical synthesis is necessary; the words themselves, through their interconnected *śakti*, directly denote the connected sentence-meaning.

#### Distractor Analysis:
- **Option B is incorrect**: Neither school claims that complex sentence-meaning is an auditory perception (*śrāvaṇa-pratyakṣa*) or an inference (*anumāna*); both treat *śabda* as an irreducible, autonomous verbal pramāṇa.
- **Option C is incorrect**: Both Kumārila Bhaṭṭa and Prabhākara are staunch Mīmāṁsakas who violently reject the Grammarians’ (Bhartṛhari’s) *sphoṭavāda*. Both maintain *varṇavāda* (that meaning is conveyed by the eternal phonemes themselves).
- **Option D is incorrect**: Neither school reduces *śakti* exclusively to verbs or nouns; the debate is not about parts of speech, but whether words denote isolated relata (*padārtha*) or connected relations (*anvita-padārtha*).

### Question 9 (Format A: Conceptual MCQ - Epistemic Status of Nirvikalpaka Pratyakṣa: Nyāya vs. Buddhism)

The transition from indeterminate perception (*nirvikalpaka-pratyakṣa*) to determinate perception (*savikalpaka-pratyakṣa*) constitutes a critical battleground between the Buddhist epistemologists (Diṅnāga and Dharmakīrti) and the Naiyāyikas (from Jayanta Bhaṭṭa to Gaṅgeśa). 

Which of the following propositions accurately reflects the **Navya-Nyāya** doctrine regarding the cognitive accessibility and structure of *nirvikalpaka-pratyakṣa*?

A) *Nirvikalpaka-pratyakṣa* is an internally luminous, directly experienced state (*svasaṁvedana*), which is subsequently structured into subject-predicate relations by verbal memory.  
B) *Nirvikalpaka-pratyakṣa* is entirely beyond direct introspective perception (*atīndriya* / *anupalabhya*); it is not apprehended by reflective apperception (*anuvyavasāya*), but is posited strictly as a necessary logical/epistemic inference (*anumeya*), because a qualified cognition (*savikalpaka-viśiṣṭa-jñāna*) logically presupposes the prior apprehension of the qualifier (*viśeṣaṇa*) without qualification.  
C) *Nirvikalpaka-pratyakṣa* is identical with the Buddhist *svalakṣaṇa*, apprehending pure unnameable efficiency (*arthakriyāsāmarthya*), which is distorted as soon as *jāti* (universal) is superimposed.  
D) *Nirvikalpaka-pratyakṣa* grasps only the bare substance (*dravya-mātra*), while the universal (*sāmānya*) and the inherence relation (*samavāya*) are supplied by the internal organ (*manas*) as mental fictions.

---

#### Correct Answer:
**B) Nirvikalpaka-pratyakṣa is entirely beyond direct introspective perception (atīndriya / anupalabhya); it is not apprehended by reflective apperception (anuvyavasāya), but is posited strictly as a necessary logical/epistemic inference (anumeya), because a qualified cognition (savikalpaka-viśiṣṭa-jñāna) logically presupposes the prior apprehension of the qualifier (viśeṣaṇa) without qualification.**

#### Philosophical Grounding & Primary Source:
In Navya-Nyāya, particularly in Gaṅgeśa's *Tattvacintāmaṇi* (Pratyakṣakhaṇḍa, *Nirvikalpakavāda*) and Annambhaṭṭa's *Tarkasaṁgraha-Dīpikā*:
- *Nirvikalpaka* is defined as: *vaiśiṣṭyānavagāhi jñānam* (cognition that does not grasp any relational qualification or relational structure between substantive, qualifier, and relation: *viśeṣya-viśeṣaṇa-sambandhānavagāhi*).
- Because all introspective apperception (*anuvyavasāya*) takes a structured, propositional form (e.g., *"I know the pot"*—*ghaṭam ahaṁ jānāmi*, where "pot-ness" qualifies the pot), *anuvyavasāya* can only register *savikalpaka* cognition. It can never turn backwards to catch *nirvikalpaka* cognition in the act.
- Therefore, how is *nirvikalpaka* proven to exist? It is established by inference (*anumāna*):
  $$\text{Viśiṣṭa-buddhiḥ viśeṣaṇa-jñāna-janyā, viśiṣṭa-buddhitvāt, daṇḍīti-buddhivat.}$$
  ("Every cognition of a qualified entity is generated by the prior cognition of the qualifier, because it is a qualified cognition, just like the cognition 'a man with a staff' presupposes the prior cognition of the staff").
  Before one can cognize "This is a pot" (*ayam ghaṭaḥ*, where pot-ness is the qualifier), one must have had an unconstructed cognition of "pot-ness" and "the object" in isolation, free of relational synthesis. That primary, unstructured cognitive stage is *nirvikalpaka*. It is real, objective, and valid, but strictly ungraspable by introspection (*atīndriya*).

#### Distractor Analysis:
- **Option A is incorrect**: Positing *svasaṁvedana* (self-intimating/self-conscious nature of cognition) is the hallmark of Yogācāra-Sautrāntika Buddhism and Prabhākara Mīmāṁsā, which Nyāya uncompromisingly rejects. For Nyāya, a cognition cannot cognize itself; it is cognized only by a subsequent act of apperception (*anuvyavasāya*).
- **Option C is incorrect**: This is the Buddhist view of Dharmakīrti (*Nyāyabindu*: *pratyakṣaṁ kalpanāpoḍham abhrāntam*). For the Buddhist, the universal (*sāmānya*) is an unreal mental construct (*vikalpa* / *apoha*). For the Naiyāyika, the universal is an eternal, objective ontological reality (*nityam ekam anekānugatam*), grasped objectively even in *nirvikalpaka*.
- **Option D is incorrect**: In Nyāya realism, neither *sāmānya* nor *samavāya* is a mental fiction. Both exist out there in the mind-independent world and are directly captured in reality by sensory contact.

### Question 10 (Format D: Comparative Matching - Theories of Error / Khyātivāda in Pramāṇa-Śāstra)

Match the epistemological systems listed in **Column I** with their precise technical characterization of the locus and nature of perceptual error (*khyātivāda*) in **Column II**, and their ontological ground in **Column III**:

| Column I (School) | Column II (Theory of Error) | Column III (Ontological Ground) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **P. Prābhākara Mīmāṁsā** | 1. *Anyathākhyātivāda* | i. The illusory object is neither real (*sat*) nor unreal (*asat*), but an ineffable positive entity (*bhāvarūpa*) generated by cosmic ignorance (*avidyā*). |
| **Q. Advaita Vedānta** | 2. *Akhyātivāda* | ii. Error consists of non-discrimination (*vivekāgraha*) between a present perceptual presentation and an awakened memory-trace. |
| **R. Nyāya** | 3. *Anirvacanīyakhyāti* | iii. An objectively real entity located elsewhere (*deśāntarīya*) is cognized through extraordinary contact (*jñānalakṣaṇa*) as qualifying a present locus. |
| **S. Mādhyamika Buddhism** | 4. *Asatkhyātivāda* | iv. Non-existent reality (*alīka*) is cognized as existent due to fundamental conceptual reification (*vikalpa / vāsanā*). |

Choose the correct matching code:
A) P-2-ii, Q-3-i, R-1-iii, S-4-iv  
B) P-1-iii, Q-2-ii, R-3-i, S-4-iv  
C) P-2-iv, Q-3-i, R-1-ii, S-4-iii  
D) P-4-iv, Q-1-i, R-2-ii, S-3-iii  

---

#### Correct Answer:
**A) P-2-ii, Q-3-i, R-1-iii, S-4-iv**

#### Philosophical Grounding & Primary Source:
1. **Prābhākara Mīmāṁsā (Akhyātivāda)**: In *Prakaraṇapañcikā*, Prabhākara asserts that error is fundamentally omission, not commission—*bhedāgraha* (non-apprehension of the difference) between two distinct cognitive events: the perception of "this" (*purovartin*) and the memory of "silver" (*smṛti*). Because all knowledge is intrinsically valid (*svataḥ-pramāṇa*), there is no misapprehension of an unreal entity, but a failure of discrimination (**P-2-ii**).
2. **Advaita Vedānta (Anirvacanīyakhyāti)**: In Śaṅkara’s *Adhyāsabhāṣya* and Prakāśātman’s *Pañcapādikā-Vivarana*, the apparent silver cannot be real (*sat*), because it is sublated by subsequent knowledge; nor can it be unreal (*asat*), because it is visually experienced; nor can it be both, because that violates non-contradiction. Hence, it is an inexplicable, apparent creation (*anirvacanīya-khyāti*) produced by *tūlāvidyā* (**Q-3-i**).
3. **Nyāya (Anyathākhyātivāda)**: In Vātsyāyana’s *Nyāyabhāṣya* and Gaṅgeśa’s *Tattvacintāmaṇi*, error is apprehension of an object as otherwise (*anyathā-prakāraka*). The silver exists elsewhere (e.g., in a shop); through memory-revival functioning as an extraordinary perceptual connection (*jñānalakṣaṇa-sannikarṣa*), that real elsewhere-silver is cognized as qualifying the real shell presented before the eyes (**R-1-iii**).
4. **Mādhyamika Buddhism (Asatkhyātivāda)**: In Mādhyamika dialectic (e.g., Candrakīrti’s *Prasannapadā*), error consists in the projection of intrinsic existence (*svabhāva*) onto phenomena that are inherently empty (*śūnya*) and non-existent as intrinsic realities (**S-4-iv**).

#### Distractor Analysis:
- Any permutation deviating from **A (P-2-ii, Q-3-i, R-1-iii, S-4-iv)** misattributes either the specific nomenclature of the *khyāti* or its distinctive metaphysical mechanism. For instance, attributing *Anyathākhyāti* to Prābhākara (B) confuses it with the Nyāya or Bhāṭṭa positions, while attributing *Asatkhyāti* to Advaita contradicts Advaita’s cardinal tenet that an absolute non-entity (*alīka*, like a hare’s horn) cannot even appear as a presentation.
