### Question 1 (Format A: Conceptual Rigor — G.E. Moore’s Open Question Argument & Naturalistic Fallacy)

In *Principia Ethica* (1903, Chapter 1), G.E. Moore formulated the celebrated **Open Question Argument** to expose the **Naturalistic Fallacy**. Consider a proposed definitional identity: *"Goodness is that which promotes the evolutionary survival of the species."* How does Moore’s argument demonstrate that this definition fails?

A) Because empirical biology has proven that evolution proceeds by random mutation rather than teleological design, making survival scientifically irrelevant to moral action.  
B) Because if "good" were identical in meaning with "promoting evolutionary survival," the question *"Is promoting the evolutionary survival of the species good?"* would be a trivial, closed analytic tautology (identical to *"Is that which promotes evolutionary survival that which promotes evolutionary survival?"*); but since the question remains intelligible, substantive, and open for rational debate, the definition fails to capture the simple, unanalyzable, non-natural property of Goodness.  
C) Because moral statements are non-cognitive emotional ejaculations that possess no descriptive meaning whatsoever, meaning that no predicate can be linked to goodness.  
D) Because Moore proved that goodness is identical to the quantitative maximization of sensory pleasure, rendering evolutionary theories superfluous.

**Correct Answer:** **B**

#### Exhaustive Philosophical Grounding & Textual Proof
In *Principia Ethica* (§§10–13), G.E. Moore argues that the central question of ethics is: *What is good in itself?* Most moral philosophers (hedonists, evolutionary naturalists, metaphysical ethicists) mistakenly believe that when they name a property that always accompanies good things (e.g., pleasure, evolutionary fitness, God's will), they have *defined* the word "good." Moore names this the **Naturalistic Fallacy** (*naturalistischer Fehlschluss*): the error of identifying the non-natural, simple, unanalyzable property "good" with any natural object or property (such as pleasure, desire, or survival).

To prove that "good" is indefinable, Moore introduces the **Open Question Argument**:
1. Take any proposed definition of "good" as natural property $N$ (e.g., $N =$ "that which promotes evolutionary survival," or $N =$ "that which causes pleasure").
2. If this definition were correct, the concept "good" and the concept "$N$" would be semantically identical, just like "bachelor" and "unmarried adult male."
3. If someone asks: *"I know John is an unmarried adult male, but is John a bachelor?"*, this is a **closed question**—it is a redundant tautology that reveals the questioner does not understand English words.
4. Now test the normative question: *"I know that action $A$ promotes the evolutionary survival of the species, but is action $A$ good?"*
5. This question is visibly **open** (*eine offene Frage*). It is not self-contradictory to doubt it, nor is it a meaningless tautology; two rational speakers can genuinely debate whether evolutionary survival is good (e.g., if survival involves cruel Social Darwinian exploitation).
6. Because the question remains open for *any* natural or metaphysical predicate $N$, "good" cannot mean the same thing as $N$. Good is a simple, unanalyzable, non-natural property, directly apprehended by intuition, analogous to the simple sensory quality "yellow" (which cannot be explained to someone who has not experienced it).

#### Distractor Analysis
- **Option A is incorrect:** It mistakes an internal semantic/conceptual argument for an external empirical biological critique. Moore’s argument works regardless of which biological theory of evolution is true.
- **Option C is incorrect:** It attributes A.J. Ayer’s radical non-cognitivism (emotivism) to G.E. Moore. Moore was an uncompromising **Moral Realist** and **Cognitivist**: he insisted that moral judgments are propositions that are objectively true or false, referring to real non-natural properties.
- **Option D is incorrect:** Moore explicitly deployed the Open Question Argument against hedonism (specifically against Bentham and Mill), demonstrating that "pleasure is good" is a synthetic claim, not an analytic definition.

**Primary Source / Textual Reference:** G.E. Moore, *Principia Ethica* (Cambridge University Press, 1903), Chapter I: "The Subject-Matter of Ethics," §§10–14, pp. 5–21.

### Question 2 (Format B: Assertion-Reason — J.S. Mill’s Qualitative Hedonism and the Competent Judges)

Given below are two statements: one is labelled as **Assertion (A)** and the other is labelled as **Reason (R)**.

- **Assertion (A):** In *Utilitarianism* (1861, Chapter 2), John Stuart Mill departs from Jeremy Bentham’s strictly quantitative hedonism by asserting that intellectual and moral pleasures are qualitatively superior to mere bodily sensations of pleasure, famously declaring: *"It is better to be a human being dissatisfied than a pig satisfied; better to be Socrates dissatisfied than a fool satisfied."*
- **Reason (R):** Mill justifies this qualitative hierarchy by establishing the empirical criterion of the **Competent Judges**: those who have equal acquaintance with and capacity for appreciating both higher intellectual pleasures and lower sensual pleasures will consistently give a marked preference to the mode of existence that employs their higher faculties, even if it entails a greater degree of discontent.

In light of the above statements, choose the correct answer:
A) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).  
B) Both (A) and (R) are true, but (R) is NOT the correct explanation of (A).  
C) (A) is true, but (R) is false.  
D) (A) is false, but (R) is true.

**Correct Answer:** **A**

#### Exhaustive Philosophical Grounding & Textual Proof
In *Utilitarianism* (Chapter 2, "What Utilitarianism Is"), J.S. Mill confronts the widespread Victorian objection that utilitarianism is a "doctrine worthy only of swine" because it posits pleasure as the ultimate end of life.

1. **Bentham's Quantitative Stance:** Jeremy Bentham held that "quantity of pleasure being equal, push-pin is as good as poetry." He evaluated pleasure solely on quantitative metrics (intensity, duration, certainty, propinquity, etc.).
2. **Mill's Qualitative Innovation:** In **Assertion (A)**, Mill introduces a qualitative distinction: pleasures of the intellect, of feelings and imagination, and of moral sentiments possess a higher value *as pleasures* than pleasures of mere physical sensation.
3. **The Proof and Competent Judges:** How can quality of pleasure be proven without appealing to an external, non-utilitarian standard? In **Reason (R)**, Mill introduces his famous epistemological test: the **Competent Judges** (*judices competentes*). 
   - If between two pleasures, there is one to which all or almost all who have experienced both give a decided preference, irrespective of any feeling of moral obligation to prefer it, that is the more desirable pleasure.
   - Mill claims that no intelligent human being would consent to be turned into an animal/fool for the promise of the fullest indulgence in animal pleasures. Why? Because of a sense of dignity (*dignity*) inherent to human beings. Even though higher faculties require more to make them happy and make them capable of more acute suffering, the competent judge never truly wishes to sink into what they feel to be a lower grade of existence. Thus, (R) provides the exact philosophical demonstration and correct explanation for (A).

#### Distractor Analysis
- **Option B is incorrect:** (R) directly explains (A); the Competent Judges test is Mill's explicit and only methodological argument for establishing the qualitative superiority of intellectual pleasures.
- **Option C is incorrect:** (R) is Mill's exact argument in Chapter 2 of *Utilitarianism*.
- **Option D is incorrect:** (A) is the most celebrated quote in Mill's ethical philosophy.

**Primary Source / Textual Reference:** John Stuart Mill, *Utilitarianism* (1861), Chapter 2: "What Utilitarianism Is," paragraphs 4–8.

### Question 3 (Format C: Multi-Statement Evaluation — Kant's Categorical Imperative & Perfect vs Imperfect Duties)

In *Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals* (*Grundlegung zur Metaphysik der Sitten*, 1785, Section II), Immanuel Kant derives four archetypal duties from the **Formula of Universal Law** (*Universelle Gesetzformel*). Consider the following propositions:

I. A **Perfect Duty** (*vollkommene Pflicht*) admits of no exceptions in favor of inclination; violating a perfect duty (such as making a deceitful promise to get money) results in a **Contradiction in Conception** (*Widerspruch im Denken*), because the maxim, when universalized, annihilates the very possibility of the institution of promising itself.  
II. An **Imperfect Duty** (*unvollkommene Pflicht*) allows a latitude of choice in how and when it is fulfilled; violating an imperfect duty (such as neglecting one's natural talents or refusing to aid others in distress) does not result in a contradiction in conception, but in a **Contradiction in the Will** (*Widerspruch im Wollen*), because a rational agent cannot consistently will a world where nobody ever receives assistance or cultivates capacities.  
III. Kant holds that the **Formula of Humanity as an End in Itself** (*Menschheitsformel*) forbids using any rational being *merely as a means* (*bloß als Mittel*), but permits using another person as a means provided they can simultaneously contain in themselves the end of that very action (e.g., in mutually consenting cooperative transactions).  
IV. According to Kant, making a false promise to an axe-murderer searching for an innocent friend is morally permissible because the murderer’s evil intention temporarily suspends the Categorical Imperative into a hypothetical imperative.

Which of the above statements are **CORRECT**?

A) I, II, and III only  
B) I and II only  
C) II, III, and IV only  
D) I, II, III, and IV

**Correct Answer:** **A**

#### Exhaustive Philosophical Grounding & Textual Proof
- **Statements I and II are correct:** In *Grundlegung* (Ak. 4:421–424), Kant categorizes duties into perfect (strict/narrow) and imperfect (broad/meritorious) duties, toward oneself and toward others:
  1. *Contradiction in Conception (Perfect Duties):* A maxim cannot even be thought as a universal law of nature without self-contradiction. For the false promise: if everyone made lying promises when in financial distress, nobody would believe any promise; promising itself would become impossible. Thus, the maxim destroys itself in conception. Perfect duties are strict: no exceptions for inclination (e.g., duty not to commit suicide; duty not to make false promises).
  2. *Contradiction in the Will (Imperfect Duties):* A world where nobody cultivates talents or nobody helps others *could* conceivably exist (like South Sea islanders living in indolence). But a rational being *cannot consistently will* such a world without self-contradiction. A rational will necessarily wills its own preservation and development; in willing to never be helped, it deprives itself of all future means of assistance when in need. Thus, the will contradicts itself.
- **Statement III is correct:** In *Grundlegung* (Ak. 4:429), the Formula of Humanity states: "Act that you use humanity, whether in your own person or in the person of any other, always at the same time as an end, never *merely* as a means." Kant does not say we can *never* use someone as a means (we use a postal carrier or a taxi driver as a means to deliver mail or travel); the moral prohibition is using them *merely* (*bloß*) as a means—i.e., without their consent, deceiving them, or treating them as an instrument incapable of sharing the end of the action.
- **Statement IV is incorrect:** This directly contradicts Kant’s notorious essay *On a Supposed Right to Tell Lies from Benevolent Motives* (*Über ein vermeintes Recht aus Menschenliebe zu lügen*, 1797). Kant insists, in uncompromising deontological rigor, that truthfulness is a sacred, unconditional command of reason that admits of no exceptions, even when an assassin at the door asks whether our friend is hiding inside. A Categorical Imperative can *never* be suspended or degraded into a hypothetical imperative under any circumstances.

#### Distractor Analysis
- **Option B is incorrect:** It omits Statement III, which is the foundational and nuanced textual interpretation of Kant's *Selbstzweckformel* (widely emphasized by modern Kant scholars like Christine Korsgaard and Onora O'Neill).
- **Option C is incorrect:** It includes the false Statement IV.
- **Option D is incorrect:** It accepts Statement IV, falling into the common misconception that Kant would allow a consequentialist exception for lying to an axe-murderer.

**Primary Source / Textual Reference:** Immanuel Kant, *Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals* (1785), Ak. 4:421–430; and *On a Supposed Right to Tell Lies from Benevolent Motives* (1797), Ak. 8:425–430.

### Question 4 (Format A: Conceptual Distinction — J.L. Mackie’s Error Theory and the Argument from Queerness)

In *Ethics: Inventing Right and Wrong* (1977, Chapter 1), J.L. Mackie defends an ontological **Error Theory**. What are the two distinct dimensions of his famous **Argument from Queerness**, and what is the relationship between ordinary moral language and moral ontology in his theory?

A) Moral language is purely expressivist/non-cognitive; the argument from queerness states that emotions are biologically queer compared to rational calculations.  
B) Ordinary moral language is cognitivist and embeds a claim to objective prescriptivity (we speak as if moral values are objective facts); however, this claim is systematically false because: (1) *Metaphysically*, objective moral values would be entities or relations of a very strange sort, utterly different from anything else in the universe, having an intrinsic "to-be-pursuedness" built into them; and (2) *Epistemologically*, if we were aware of them, it would have to be by a totally mysterious, occult faculty of moral intuition, utterly different from all our normal ways of knowing empirical facts.  
C) Mackie argues that moral values are objectively real transcendent Platonic Forms, but that human language is too queer to describe them accurately.  
D) The argument from queerness states that homosexual behavior is unnatural under Thomistic natural law, and therefore moral errors are psychological perversions.

**Correct Answer:** **B**

#### Exhaustive Philosophical Grounding & Textual Proof
In *Ethics: Inventing Right and Wrong* (1977, Chapter 1), John L. Mackie lays the foundation for contemporary moral anti-realism:

1. **The Double Thesis of Error Theory:**
   - **Linguistic/Conceptual Claim:** Mackie agrees with moral realists against emotivists and prescriptivists. Everyday moral discourse is unmistakably **cognitivist**: when ordinary people say "Stealing is wrong," they do not mean merely "Boo to stealing!" or "I command you not to steal." They intend to refer to an objective, authoritative moral fact that exists independently of human desires—what Mackie calls **objective prescriptivity**.
   - **Ontological Claim:** But in reality, there are **no** objective values, no moral facts, and no moral properties in the universe.
   - **Conclusion:** Therefore, because ordinary moral judgments claim to describe objective values that do not exist, all positive moral judgments are systematically **false** (hence an "Error Theory," directly analogous to an atheist's view of religious discourse).
2. **The Argument from Queerness (§9):** Mackie supports his ontological claim with two prongs:
   - **Metaphysical Queerness:** If there were objective values, they would have to be entities, qualities, or relations of a wildly bizarre kind. An objective good would have to be an objective property of an action that, merely upon being perceived by an agent, immediately and intrinsically motivates the agent to pursue it (*intrinsic to-be-pursuedness*). No physical, chemical, or psychological entity in the natural world possesses such a magical property.
   - **Epistemological Queerness:** If we were to know such queer properties, how could we know them? Neither sensory perception nor standard mathematical/logical deduction could detect them. We would have to posit an occult, mysterious faculty of "moral intuition" (*moralischer Spürsinn*), which is an ad hoc philosophical fiction.

#### Distractor Analysis
- **Option A is incorrect:** It confuses Mackie’s error theory with non-cognitivism (Ayer/Stevenson). Mackie repeatedly emphasizes that moral discourse is *not* non-cognitive; it makes an objective truth-claim, but that claim fails.
- **Option C is incorrect:** Mackie is an anti-realist who vigorously denies the existence of Platonic Forms.
- **Option D is incorrect:** It conflates the technical philosophical term "queerness" (*strange / bizarre ontological status*) with a colloquial slur, committing an egregious philosophical misreading.

**Primary Source / Textual Reference:** J.L. Mackie, *Ethics: Inventing Right and Wrong* (Penguin Books, 1977), Chapter 1: "The Status of Ethics," §§7–9, pp. 30–42.

### Question 5 (Format B: Assertion-Reason — W.D. Ross on Prima Facie Duties vs Actual Duty)

Given below are two statements: one is labelled as **Assertion (A)** and the other is labelled as **Reason (R)**.

- **Assertion (A):** In *The Right and the Good* (1930), W.D. Ross rejects both Kant’s absolutist deontology and G.E. Moore’s "ideal utilitarianism" by introducing a pluralistic framework of **Prima Facie Duties** (*prima facie* obligations) that can conflict in concrete situations.
- **Reason (R):** For Ross, while our knowledge that a general duty (such as fidelity or non-maleficence) is a *prima facie* duty is self-evident and known by rational intuition (*a priori*), our determination of our **Actual Duty** (*duty proper / duty sans phrase*) in a concrete situation where duties conflict is never self-evident or certain, but is a matter of fallible moral judgment (*judgment / phronesis*).

In light of the above statements, choose the correct answer:
A) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).  
B) Both (A) and (R) are true, but (R) is NOT the correct explanation of (A).  
C) (A) is true, but (R) is false.  
D) (A) is false, but (R) is true.

**Correct Answer:** **A**

#### Exhaustive Philosophical Grounding & Textual Proof
In *The Right and the Good* (1930, Chapter 2: "What Makes Right Acts Right?"), W.D. Ross formulates an influential middle path in normative ethics:

1. **Critique of Monism:**
   - *Against Moore’s Ideal Utilitarianism:* Ross argues that utilitarianism ignores the highly personal character of duty. When I make a promise to someone, the obligation to keep it does not arise from whether breaking it produces slightly more aggregate future utility; it arises from a past transaction. Utilitarianism looks solely to the future, reducing our relations to others to that of mere beneficiaries.
   - *Against Kant’s Absolutism:* Kant claimed moral rules (e.g., do not lie) admit of no exceptions. But real life presents genuine moral dilemmas (e.g., lying to save a life).
2. **The Prima Facie Solution (Assertion A):** Ross defines a *prima facie* duty (more accurately, a conditional or characteristic duty) as a moral reason that tends to make an act right, other things being equal. He lists seven fundamental *prima facie* duties: Fidelity, Reparation, Gratitude, Justice, Beneficence, Self-improvement, and Non-maleficence (with Non-maleficence having general priority over Beneficence).
3. **The Epistemological Asymmetry (Reason R):** How do we know these duties?
   - The *general principles* of *prima facie* duties are **self-evident** (*a priori* intuition), just like the axioms of mathematics or logic. That promise-keeping is *prima facie* right requires no external proof.
   - However, in a concrete dilemma, two or more *prima facie* duties pull in opposite directions (e.g., keeping a promise vs relieving acute agony). Here, our **Actual Duty** (*duty proper* / *duty sans phrase*) is *not* self-evident. We have no algorithm or mechanical calculus. We must weigh the conflicting reasons using practical wisdom (*judgment*). Ross quotes Aristotle: "the decision rests with perception" (*en te aisthesei he krisis*). We can only form a considered, fallible opinion. Thus, (R) provides the exact epistemological foundation explaining Ross's pluralistic system in (A).

#### Distractor Analysis
- **Option B is incorrect:** (R) directly explains (A) by clarifying how Ross solves the problem of conflicting moral claims that crippled Kant’s absolutism and Moore’s monism.
- **Option C is incorrect:** (R) accurately captures Ross’s famous epistemological distinction between the certainty of *prima facie* axioms and the fallibility of actual duty judgments (*The Right and the Good*, pp. 29–33).
- **Option D is incorrect:** (A) is the central historical thesis of Ross's masterpiece.

**Primary Source / Textual Reference:** W.D. Ross, *The Right and the Good* (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1930), Chapter 2: "What Makes Right Acts Right?", pp. 16–47.

### Question 6 (Format A: Conceptual Synthesis — R.M. Hare’s Universal Prescriptivism)

In *The Language of Morals* (1952) and *Freedom and Reason* (1963), R.M. Hare developed **Universal Prescriptivism**. How does Hare overcome the charges of irrationalism leveled against A.J. Ayer’s emotivism, and how does **Universalizability** operate within his meta-ethical logic?

A) Hare proves that moral statements are analytic tautologies derived from Aristotelian category theory.  
B) Hare argues that moral judgments are not mere psychological expressions of feeling, but are **prescriptions** that structurally entail imperatives (e.g., *"You ought to do X"* logically entails *"Do X!"*); furthermore, moral judgments are governed by the logical rule of **Universalizability**: an agent who asserts a moral judgment in a specific situation is logically committed to making the identical judgment in all counterfactually identical situations, including those where the agent occupies the position of the victim.  
C) Hare maintains that prescriptivism is a form of Christian divine command theory where imperatives are issued directly by God through biblical scripture.  
D) Hare collapses moral reasoning into empirical neuroscience, demonstrating that prescriptivity is caused by prefrontal dopamine surges.

**Correct Answer:** **B**

#### Exhaustive Philosophical Grounding & Textual Proof
In *The Language of Morals* (1952) and *Freedom and Reason* (1963), R.M. Hare created a sophisticated non-cognitivist alternative to both Ayer’s crude emotivism and Moore’s intuitionism.

1. **Critique of Emotivism:** A.J. Ayer’s emotivism reduced moral statements to subjective emotional shrieks ("Boo/Hurrah!"), which made rational moral argument appear impossible. For Ayer, moral debates were merely psychological tugs-of-war.
2. **Prescriptivity:** Hare demonstrates that moral language belongs to the category of **prescriptive language**. The function of evaluative words (like "good" or "ought") is to guide conduct. A genuine moral judgment entails an imperative: if an agent sincerely says, "I ought to do $X$," the agent must logically assent to the imperative "Let me do $X$." If the agent fails to act (without physical impediment or psychological incapacity), the agent is guilty of insincerity or pragmatic contradiction.
3. **Universalizability:** This is the cornerstone of Hare’s logic of moral discourse. "Ought" is governed by formal logical constraints. To make an evaluative judgment about a particular act is *ipso facto* to commit oneself to the universal principle that anyone in a relevantly similar situation ought to do the same.
   - If I say "You ought not to break your promise to me," I cannot, on pain of logical inconsistency, say "It is permissible for me to break my promise to you in the exact same circumstances."
   - Universalizability forces the moral agent to engage in **imaginative role-reversal**: the agent must test whether they can prescribe the action when hypothetically occupying the shoes of every affected party (including the victim). Hare brilliantly demonstrates that this formal logical property of moral terms leads directly to a form of utilitarian normative outcome without presupposing naturalist metaphysics.

#### Distractor Analysis
- **Option A is incorrect:** Moral statements are synthetic and prescriptive for Hare, not analytic tautologies.
- **Option C is incorrect:** Prescriptivism is an autonomous, secular linguistic-logical analysis of moral concepts, having nothing to do with theological divine command theory.
- **Option D is incorrect:** It commits the naturalistic fallacy by reducing logical and semantic relations between speech acts to neurobiological causes.

**Primary Source / Textual Reference:** R.M. Hare, *The Language of Morals* (Oxford University Press, 1952), Part I & III; and *Freedom and Reason* (Oxford University Press, 1963), Chapters 1–3, pp. 1–50.

### Question 7 (Format C: Multi-Statement Evaluation — Alasdair MacIntyre’s After Virtue)

In *After Virtue: A Study in Moral Theory* (1981), Alasdair MacIntyre diagnoses the state of contemporary moral discourse. Consider the following propositions:

I. MacIntyre opens with a "disquieting suggestion": contemporary moral language consists of fragmented, incommensurable linguistic survivals of older moral traditions whose foundational teleological contexts have been completely obliterated.  
II. He argues that modern secular culture is thoroughly **emotivist**, because while moral judgments *pretend* to appeal to objective, impersonal standards, they function sociologically as nothing more than the manipulation of others to express personal preferences.  
III. MacIntyre identifies the fatal catastrophe of the Enlightenment project (Kierkegaard, Kant, Diderot, Hume) as its shared rejection of the classical Aristotelian teleological schema, which harmonized: (1) "man-as-he-happens-to-be", (2) "the precepts of rational ethics", and (3) "man-as-he-could-be-if-he-realized-his-essential-nature".  
IV. To reconstruct virtue ethics, MacIntyre defines a **"Practice"** (*praxis*) as any coherent, complex form of socially established cooperative human activity through which **internal goods** (*bona interna*) are realized, distinguishing them sharply from **external goods** (such as money, power, and prestige).

Which of the above statements are **CORRECT**?

A) I and II only  
B) I, III, and IV only  
C) II, III, and IV only  
D) I, II, III, and IV

**Correct Answer:** **D**

#### Exhaustive Philosophical Grounding & Textual Proof
- **Statement I is correct:** In Chapter 1, MacIntyre constructs a thought experiment where natural science is destroyed by a catastrophe and later reconstructed from torn scraps of textbooks and damaged instruments. He asserts that this is precisely what has happened to morality: we possess the language of virtue, duty, and rights, but we have lost the systematic theoretical and practical context from which they derived meaning.
- **Statement II is correct:** In Chapters 2 and 3, MacIntyre shows that modern society has institutionalized emotivism. Figures like the Manager, the Therapist, and the Rich Aesthete embody this: people treat moral debates as endless screaming matches between incommensurable premises, reducing human interaction to manipulative power plays.
- **Statement III is correct:** In Chapter 5 ("Why the Enlightenment Project of Justifying Morality Had to Fail"), MacIntyre reveals the threefold Aristotelian structure:
  1. *Untutored human nature* ("man-as-he-happens-to-be").
  2. *Rational ethics* (precepts that instruct human beings on how to transition).
  3. *Telos* ("man-as-he-could-be-if-he-realized-his-telos").
  When the Enlightenment eliminated teleology (discarding human nature’s essential purpose/end), it left ethics with only two components: untutored human desires and moral rules. But moral rules were designed precisely to discipline untutored desires in service of the *telos*! Divorced from teleology, the rules became arbitrary and impossible to rationally justify.
- **Statement IV is correct:** In Chapter 14 ("The Nature of the Virtues"), MacIntyre grounds virtue in a three-stage core conception: **Practice**, the **Narrative Unity of Life**, and **Tradition**. He defines a **Practice** (e.g., chess, farming, architecture, musical performance) as an activity where **internal goods** can only be achieved by participating in the practice and submitting to its standards of excellence. Virtues are those acquired human qualities that allow us to achieve goods internal to practices. External goods (money, fame, status) can be gained through cheating; internal goods can never be gained through cheating.

#### Distractor Analysis
All options omitting any of Statements I, II, III, or IV are incomplete. All four statements represent faithful, precise textual distillations of MacIntyre's argument in *After Virtue*.

**Primary Source / Textual Reference:** Alasdair MacIntyre, *After Virtue: A Study in Moral Theory* (University of Notre Dame Press, 1981; 3rd ed. 2007), Chapters 1, 2, 5, and 14.

### Question 8 (Format A: Conceptual Distinction — C.L. Stevenson’s Emotivism and Disagreement in Attitude)

In *Ethics and Language* (1944), Charles L. Stevenson advanced a sophisticated version of emotivism that distinguished between two types of moral disagreement. What is the fundamental difference between **Disagreement in Belief** and **Disagreement in Attitude**, and how do **Persuasive Definitions** function in Stevenson's meta-ethics?

A) Disagreement in belief concerns logical syntax, while disagreement in attitude concerns grammatical semantics; persuasive definitions are dictionary entries written by academic linguists.  
B) Disagreement in belief involves conflicting factual or scientific beliefs about the empirical properties of an action (resolvable by empirical evidence); disagreement in attitude involves conflicting psychological desires, evaluations, or dispositions to be for or against something; persuasive definitions operate by keeping the positive emotive meaning of a term intact while surreptitiously changing its descriptive meaning to redirect the interlocutor’s attitudes.  
C) Disagreement in belief occurs between atheists and theists, while disagreement in attitude occurs within monastic orders; persuasive definitions are legal statutes enacted by parliament.  
D) Disagreement in attitude can be resolved by mathematical deduction, while disagreement in belief requires sensory hypnosis.

**Correct Answer:** **B**

#### Exhaustive Philosophical Grounding & Textual Proof
In *Ethics and Language* (1944, Chapters 1–3 & 9), C.L. Stevenson significantly elevated emotivism above A.J. Ayer's basic formulations:

1. **The Dual Dimension of Disagreement:**
   - **Disagreement in Belief:** Two parties hold mutually exclusive factual beliefs. Party $A$ believes capital punishment deters violent crime; Party $B$ believes statistics show it does not deter crime. This is an empirical disagreement. In principle, scientific investigation, criminology, and evidence can resolve it.
   - **Disagreement in Attitude:** Two parties have conflicting emotional orientations, desires, or evaluative stances (*pro* and *con* attitudes). Even if $A$ and $B$ agree on *all* factual data (e.g., agreeing that execution has zero deterrent effect), $A$ may still demand execution out of retributive vengeance, while $B$ opposes it out of mercy. No amount of factual evidence can logically compel a convergence in attitude. Stevenson famously argues that moral arguments almost always center on disagreement in attitude.
2. **Persuasive Definitions (Chapter 9):** Stevenson coins this term to explain a common rhetorical maneuver in ethics and politics. A word like "culture," "courage," "justice," or "democracy" possesses both a **descriptive meaning** (the factual criteria) and a powerful **emotive meaning** (an aura of praise or condemnation).
   - In a **persuasive definition**, a speaker surreptitiously alters the *descriptive meaning* of the term while retaining its praised *emotive force*. 
   - Example: "True courage is not physical bravado in battle [old descriptive sense], but quiet intellectual meditation [new descriptive sense]!" The speaker uses the laudatory halo of "courage" to manipulate and steer the listener's attitude toward endorsing intellectual meditation.

#### Distractor Analysis
- **Option A is incorrect:** It trivializes Stevenson's behavioral and psychological analysis into formal syntax and lexicography.
- **Option C is incorrect:** It narrows Stevenson's universal meta-ethical theory to sectarian religious sociological settings.
- **Option D is incorrect:** It claims disagreement in attitude can be resolved by mathematical deduction, which directly contradicts Stevenson’s central thesis that attitudes are non-cognitive and cannot be deduced logically.

**Primary Source / Textual Reference:** Charles L. Stevenson, *Ethics and Language* (Yale University Press, 1944), Chapter 1: "The Nature of Ethical Disagreements" and Chapter 9: "Persuasive Definitions," pp. 1–36, 206–226.

### Question 9 (Format D: Doctrinal Matching — Foundational Meta-Ethical Theses)

Match the prominent meta-ethical position in **List-I** with its core epistemological/ontological thesis in **List-II**:

| **q. Non-Naturalist Realism (G.E. Moore)** | **2.** Moral judgments are universalizable prescriptions that entail imperatives, functioning under formal logical consistency rules. |

Choose the correct matching code:

### Question 10 (Format B: Assertion-Reason — Elizabeth Anscombe’s Critique of Modern Moral Philosophy)

Given below are two statements: one is labelled as **Assertion (A)** and the other is labelled as **Reason (R)**.

- **Assertion (A):** In her seminal 1958 paper *"Modern Moral Philosophy"*, G.E.M. Anscombe advocated that the concepts of "moral obligation", "moral duty", and the moral sense of "ought" should be completely abandoned.
- **Reason (R):** Anscombe argued that the concept of a "morally binding law" or unconditional "obligation" is an incoherent historical survival of a divine law conception of ethics; without the theological framework of a divine lawgiver, the concept of a moral law that binds the will has lost all intelligible meaning.

In light of the above statements, choose the correct answer:
A) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).  
B) Both (A) and (R) are true, but (R) is NOT the correct explanation of (A).  
C) (A) is true, but (R) is false.  
D) (A) is false, but (R) is true.

**Correct Answer:** **A**

#### Exhaustive Philosophical Grounding & Textual Proof
In *"Modern Moral Philosophy"* (*Philosophy*, Vol. 33, No. 124, January 1958), G.E.M. Anscombe delivered one of the most explosive and influential critiques in 20th-century Anglo-American philosophy, which single-handedly revived contemporary **Virtue Ethics**:

1. **The Three Theses:**
   - *First Thesis:* Moral philosophy should be abandoned until we possess an adequate philosophy of psychology (specifically concepts like action, intention, pleasure, and flourishing).
   - *Second Thesis (Assertion A):* The concepts of "moral obligation" and "moral duty"—and the special modern sense of "morally ought"—are psychological survivals that ought to be jettisoned because they are harmful or at least useless.
   - *Third Thesis:* The differences between English moral philosophers from Sidgwick to the present are insignificant; they are all consequentialists (a term Anscombe coined in this very paper).
2. **The Rationale for Abandoning Moral Obligation (Reason R):**
   - Anscombe conducts an archaeological analysis: Aristotle has no concept of "moral obligation" or "moral duty" in the modern sense; he speaks of virtue (*arete*), vice (*kakia*), and what is noble (*kalon*) or disgraceful (*aischron*).
   - The concept of "obligation" entered Western thought through the Christian, Jewish, and Stoic conception of ethics as **Divine Law**. A law requires a sovereign legislator who commands and imposes sanctions.
   - Modern secular moral philosophy (Kant, utilitarians) abandoned belief in a divine lawgiver, but desperately tried to retain the psychological force of a *law-conception of ethics*. Kant tried to make the individual will its own lawgiver ("autonomous legislation"), which Anscombe dismisses as absurd (like a person making a contract with himself: whatever he decides to do, he can release himself from).
   - Therefore, holding onto "moral obligation" without God is like holding onto the concept of a criminal verdict without a legal code and without judges. It is an empty, mesmerized ghost of a defunct theological framework. Anscombe urges a return to Aristotelian virtue terms (just, unjust, courageous, temperate) evaluated in relation to human flourishing. Thus, (R) is the exact argument and explanation for (A).

#### Distractor Analysis
- **Option B is incorrect:** (R) directly explains (A); Anscombe's historical-conceptual diagnosis of the divine law origin is the explicit proof of her second thesis.
- **Option C is incorrect:** (R) accurately captures the core thesis of Anscombe's 1958 paper.
- **Option D is incorrect:** (A) is the famous headline recommendation of Anscombe's paper.

**Primary Source / Textual Reference:** G.E.M. Anscombe, "Modern Moral Philosophy," *Philosophy*, Vol. 33, No. 124 (1958), pp. 1–19.

### Question 11 (Format A: Conceptual Distinction — Act Utilitarianism vs Rule Utilitarianism and Smart’s Critique)

In *Utilitarianism: For and Against* (1973), J.J.C. Smart mounts a devastating internal critique against **Rule Utilitarianism** (advocated by Richard Brandt). Why does Smart brand Rule Utilitarianism as a form of irrational **"Rule Worship"** (*superstitious rule worship*)?

A) Because rule utilitarianism requires strict adherence to biblical scripture rather than mathematical hedonism.  
B) Because if a rule utilitarian acknowledges that following a generally beneficial rule $R$ (e.g., "Always keep promises") in a specific emergency situation will foreseeably cause catastrophic misery, but still insists on obeying $R$ merely because it is the general rule, the agent is elevating the rule into an irrational idol, abandoning the fundamental utilitarian commitment to maximizing happiness and preventing suffering.  
C) Because rule utilitarianism is identical to Kantian deontology, and Smart was an intuitionist moral realist.  
D) Because act utilitarians calculate consequences for ten thousand years into the future, whereas rule utilitarians only calculate consequences for twenty minutes.

**Correct Answer:** **B**

#### Exhaustive Philosophical Grounding & Textual Proof
In "Extreme and Restricted Utilitarianism" (*The Philosophical Quarterly*, 1956) and *Utilitarianism: For and Against* (1973), J.J.C. Smart defends **Act Utilitarianism** (which he calls "Extreme Utilitarianism") against **Rule Utilitarianism** ("Restricted Utilitarianism").

1. **The Dilemma for Rule Utilitarianism:** Rule utilitarians argue that we should judge actions not by their individual consequences, but by whether they conform to a set of rules whose general acceptance maximizes utility.
2. **Smart's Dilemma of Collapse or Rule Worship:**
   - *Case 1 (Modifying Rules):* Suppose rule $R$ is "Do not lie." A murderer comes asking for our friend. If the rule utilitarian says, "Well, we modify the rule to: 'Do not lie except to prevent murder,' and 'Do not lie except when breaking the rule produces more utility,' then rule utilitarianism simply collapses into act utilitarianism! Every exception is factored in, making rules mere handy "rules of thumb."
   - *Case 2 (Refusing to Modify Rules):* Suppose the rule utilitarian refuses to make exceptions, insisting that rule $R$ must be obeyed even when everyone knows that obeying it in this specific instance will lead to horrific suffering and zero utility. Smart asks: *Why should a utilitarian praise this?* To refuse to prevent misery out of blind allegiance to a general rule is to transform a rule—which was originally meant to be a means to human happiness—into an end in itself. Smart calls this **"superstitious rule worship"**: the rule utilitarian has abandoned the core utilitarian principle (human welfare) and become an irrational dogmatist.

#### Distractor Analysis
- **Option A is incorrect:** Rule utilitarianism is a secular consequentialist theory, not a religious scriptural code.
- **Option C is incorrect:** Smart is an uncompromising utilitarian consequentialist, not an intuitionist. While rule utilitarianism shares superficial similarities with deontology, Smart’s critique is specifically designed to show that rule utilitarianism cannot be justified *on utilitarian grounds*.
- **Option D is incorrect:** Time horizons have nothing to do with the structural logical critique Smart makes regarding the status of moral rules.

**Primary Source / Textual Reference:** J.J.C. Smart and Bernard Williams, *Utilitarianism: For and Against* (Cambridge University Press, 1973), Chapter 2: "Act-utilitarianism and rule-utilitarianism," pp. 9–12; and J.J.C. Smart, "Extreme and Restricted Utilitarianism," *The Philosophical Quarterly*, Vol. 6, No. 25 (1956), pp. 344–354.

### Question 12 (Format C: Multi-Statement Evaluation — Aristotle’s Moral Psychology: Akrasia and Phronesis)

In Book VII of the *Nicomachean Ethics* (1145a–1152a), Aristotle investigates **Akrasia** (incontinence / weakness of will). Consider the following propositions regarding Aristotle’s resolution of the Socratic paradox:

I. Socrates (in Plato's *Protagoras*) famously denied the existence of *akrasia*, asserting that no one does wrong willingly, and that whenever someone appears to succumb to pleasure, they are merely suffering from intellectual ignorance (*amathia*).  
II. Aristotle partially defends Socrates by arguing that the incontinent person does not act against fully realized, actualized knowledge in the moment of action; rather, the agent’s knowledge is latent, inactive, or clouded by bodily passions (analogous to someone who is asleep, mad, or drunk).  
III. Aristotle explains *akrasia* using the **Practical Syllogism**: the agent possesses a universal major premise (e.g., "Sweet things are unhealthy") and a minor premise (e.g., "This cake is sweet"), but passion (*epithumia*) activates a competing universal premise ("Everything sweet is pleasant"), causing the agent to act on desire while the universal premise of reason remains functionally disabled.  
IV. Aristotle concludes that *akrasia* is identical to *akolasia* (vicious intemperance), because both agents experience zero regret after performing a shameful act.

Which of the above statements are **CORRECT**?

A) I and II only  
B) I, II, and III only  
C) II, III, and IV only  
D) I, III, and IV only

**Correct Answer:** **B**

#### Exhaustive Philosophical Grounding & Textual Proof
- **Statement I is correct:** In *Protagoras* (352b–358d), Socrates maintains that "Virtue is Knowledge" (*arete is episteme*). If a person truly knows what is best, it is impossible for them to choose the worse; reason cannot be "dragged around like a slave" by appetites. What men call "being overcome by pleasure" is simply an error in the art of measurement—a failure of knowledge.
- **Statement II is correct:** In *Nicomachean Ethics* (Book VII, Chapter 3), Aristotle refuses to dismiss *akrasia* as mere illusion, because everyday experience demonstrates people acting against their better judgments. However, to resolve the paradox, Aristotle distinguishes between two senses of "knowing":
  1. Having knowledge without exercising it (dormant/potential knowledge).
  2. Actively attending to and using knowledge.
  The *akrates* (incontinent person) has knowledge only in the dormant sense. Passion (*pathos*) physically alters the body and clouds the intellect, so that the agent recites moral words mechanically without their meaning penetrating the soul, like an actor on stage or a drunken person reciting Empedocles.
- **Statement III is correct:** Aristotle models this via the **Practical Syllogism** (1147a25–1147b19):
  - Universal premise 1 (Reason): "Tasting sweet things is harmful to health."
  - Universal premise 2 (Appetite): "Everything sweet is pleasant to taste."
  - Particular premise (Perception): "This particular object is sweet."
  - When appetite (*epithumia*) is strong, it drives the body toward the particular object under the banner of Universal Premise 2, while the major premise of Reason (Premise 1) is blocked from practical deployment.
- **Statement IV is completely incorrect:** Aristotle draws a sharp, vital distinction between the **incontinent person** (*akrates*) and the **intemperate/vicious person** (*akolastos*). The *akolastos* (vicious person) acts from deliberate choice (*prohairesis*); he believes that pursuing extreme physical pleasures is the right way to live, and he feels **no regret** (*metameleia*). The *akrates*, by contrast, knows his act is bad, acts against his rational principle under the surge of desire, and immediately experiences **deep regret and remorse**. Therefore, *akrasia* is curable, whereas intemperance (*akolasia*) is incurable!

#### Distractor Analysis
- **Option A is incorrect:** It omits Statement III, which is Aristotle's famous technical contribution to the logic of moral action.
- **Option C is incorrect:** It includes the false Statement IV.
- **Option D is incorrect:** It includes Statement IV while omitting Statement II.

**Primary Source / Textual Reference:** Aristotle, *Nicomachean Ethics*, Book VII, Chapters 1–3, 1145a15–1147b20, 1150a20–1151a10.

### Question 13 (Format A: Conceptual Synthesis — Philippa Foot and the Trolley Problem)

In her foundational 1967 paper *"The Problem of Abortion and the Doctrine of the Double Effect"*, Philippa Foot introduced the thought experiment now known as the **Trolley Problem**. How does Foot resolve the moral difference between the **Runaway Trolley** (switching the track to kill one person instead of five) and the **Transplant Surgeon** (killing one healthy patient to harvest their organs to save five dying patients)?

A) Foot argues that consequentialism is correct in both cases, and that the surgeon should kill the healthy patient immediately.  
B) Foot distinguishes between **Negative Duties** (the duty to refrain from injuring or killing someone) and **Positive Duties** (the duty to provide aid or save lives); in the Trolley case, the driver faces a conflict between two negative duties (killing one vs killing five), so minimizing the violation of negative duty is permissible; whereas in the Transplant case, the surgeon faces a conflict between a positive duty (to save five lives) and a negative duty (not to kill the healthy patient), and our negative duty to refrain from killing strictly overrides our positive duty to render aid.  
C) Foot claims that the Doctrine of Double Effect permits organ harvesting as long as the surgeon does not feel psychological hatred toward the healthy victim.  
D) Foot proves that trains possess inherent metaphysical rights, while operating rooms are governed by Kant's kingdom of ends.

**Correct Answer:** **B**

#### Exhaustive Philosophical Grounding & Textual Proof
In *"The Problem of Abortion and the Doctrine of the Double Effect"* (*Oxford Review*, No. 5, 1967), Philippa Foot inaugurated the modern trolley literature:

1. **The Apparent Paradox:**
   - *Case 1 (Trolley):* A runaway tram is hurtling down a track toward five workers. The driver can steer the tram onto a spur track, killing one worker instead of five. Almost everyone agrees this is permissible.
   - *Case 2 (Transplant):* A surgeon has five patients dying of different organ failures. A healthy visitor enters the clinic for a routine checkup. The surgeon could painlessly kill the visitor, harvest organs, and save the five patients. Almost everyone agrees this is monstrous murder.
   - *The Paradox:* In both cases, the net mathematical outcome is the same: one life traded for five. Why is it permissible in the trolley case, but forbidden in the hospital case?
2. **Foot's Resolution: Positive vs Negative Duties:**
   Foot rejects the traditional Catholic Doctrine of the Double Effect as the sole explanation and introduces a distinction between:
   - **Negative Duties:** Duties to avoid doing injury, killing, or encroaching on others (*non-maleficence*).
   - **Positive Duties:** Duties to render aid, provide sustenance, or rescue (*beneficence*).
   - *Priority Rule:* In general, **negative duties have far greater moral weight than positive duties**. We cannot fulfill a positive duty to help person $B$ by directly violating our negative duty not to kill person $A$.
   - *Application to Transplant:* The surgeon wants to fulfill a positive duty (save five). To do so, the surgeon must initiate a fatal assault on an innocent person, violating a severe negative duty (do not kill). The negative duty strictly overrides the positive duty.
   - *Application to Trolley:* In the runaway trolley, the driver is *already* steering a lethal vehicle that is hurtling toward death. Whichever track the driver chooses, people will be killed. The conflict is between **two negative duties**: killing five versus killing one. Faced with an unavoidable clash between two negative duties, it is rational and permissible to choose the lesser evil: killing one rather than five.

#### Distractor Analysis
- **Option A is incorrect:** It represents act utilitarianism, which Foot's paper was explicitly designed to refute.
- **Option C is incorrect:** It distorts the Doctrine of Double Effect into mere psychological emotion; the DDE strictly requires proportional necessity and that the evil effect is not the means to the good end.
- **Option D is incorrect:** It is nonsensical gibberish.

**Primary Source / Textual Reference:** Philippa Foot, "The Problem of Abortion and the Doctrine of the Double Effect," *The Oxford Review*, No. 5 (1967), pp. 5–15; reprinted in *Virtues and Vices and Other Essays in Moral Philosophy* (Blackwell, 1978).

### Question 14 (Format B: Assertion-Reason — Simon Blackburn’s Quasi-Realism and Projectivism)

Given below are two statements: one is labelled as **Assertion (A)** and the other is labelled as **Reason (R)**.

- **Assertion (A):** In *Spreading the Word* (1984) and *Ruling Passions* (1998), Simon Blackburn develops **Quasi-Realism**, arguing that an anti-realist expressivist can legitimately "earn the right" to use the entire realistic vocabulary of ordinary moral discourse (e.g., claiming that moral statements can be "true", "false", "facts", or "mind-independent").
- **Reason (R):** For Blackburn’s projectivism, moral values do not exist in the external metaphysical furniture of the universe; rather, human beings project their non-cognitive attitudes and emotional desires onto the world, but through a systematic recursive logic of attitudes, we develop a normative framework that functions with truth-evaluable propositional grammar without incurring any metaphysical commitment to queer non-natural properties.

In light of the above statements, choose the correct answer:
A) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).  
B) Both (A) and (R) are true, but (R) is NOT the correct explanation of (A).  
C) (A) is true, but (R) is false.  
D) (A) is false, but (R) is true.

**Correct Answer:** **A**

#### Exhaustive Philosophical Grounding & Textual Proof
In *Spreading the Word* (1984, Chapters 5 & 6) and *Ruling Passions* (1998), Simon Blackburn introduces **Quasi-Realism**:

1. **The Challenge for Expressivism:** Classic expressivism/emotivism (Ayer) was plagued by the Frege-Geach problem (how moral terms function in unasserted contexts like conditionals: "If lying is wrong, then getting your little brother to lie is wrong"). Realists argued that only moral realism can explain why moral language behaves like truth-apt propositional logic.
2. **The Quasi-Realist Program (Assertion A):** Blackburn refuses to surrender to realism. He asks: *Can an anti-realist expressivist explain and justify all the surface features of moral realism without accepting moral realist ontology?* Yes! The quasi-realist seeks to "earn the right" to talk of moral truths, moral knowledge, moral mistakes, and objective facts, completely from an expressivist starting point.
3. **Projectivism and Attitude Logic (Reason R):** 
   - Blackburn adopts David Hume’s metaphor: the mind has "a great propensity to spread itself on external objects." We experience natural events and react with emotional attitudes (approval, disgust, indignation). We then **project** these attitudes onto the world, speaking as if the world itself possessed moral qualities.
   - Blackburn constructs a formal logic of attitudes ($E$-attitudes and $H$-attitudes) to show how complex normative discourse evolves. 
   - When we say "Torturing children is objectively wrong, even if everyone approved of it," the quasi-realist explains this not as a metaphysical claim about Platonic realm entities, but as the expression of a second-order attitude: we have an attitude of profound disapproval toward any scenario where people approve of torturing children. Thus, projectivism explains moral realism's grammar while avoiding J.L. Mackie’s "queer" non-natural ontology. Therefore, (R) provides the exact philosophical mechanism and correct explanation for (A).

#### Distractor Analysis
- **Option B is incorrect:** (R) directly explains (A); quasi-realism is literally the philosophical program of using projectivism to justify realist semantics.
- **Option C is incorrect:** (R) accurately captures Blackburn’s projectivist mechanics.
- **Option D is incorrect:** (A) is the defining thesis of Simon Blackburn’s contribution to contemporary meta-ethics.

**Primary Source / Textual Reference:** Simon Blackburn, *Spreading the Word* (Oxford University Press, 1984), Chapter 6; and *Ruling Passions: A Theory of Practical Reasoning* (Oxford University Press, 1998), Chapters 3 & 9.

### Question 15 (Format A: Conceptual Counter-Argument — Bernard Williams's Critique of Utilitarian Integrity)

In his contribution to *Utilitarianism: For and Against* (1973), Bernard Williams presents the famous cases of **George the Chemist** and **Jim and the Indians**. What fundamental philosophical objection against utilitarianism does Williams establish through these examples?

A) Utilitarianism is unacceptable because it relies on outdated Newtonian physics rather than quantum mechanics.  
B) Utilitarianism alienates an agent from their own moral feelings, life-defining commitments, and projects, destroying the agent’s **Integrity** by operating under a doctrine of "negative responsibility" wherein an agent is held just as responsible for what they fail to prevent others from doing as for what they do themselves.  
C) Utilitarianism is flawed because it fails to calculate the monetary inflation of economic welfare.  
D) Utilitarianism is incompatible with Christian salvation because it denies the existence of purgatory.

**Correct Answer:** **B**

#### Exhaustive Philosophical Grounding & Textual Proof
In *Utilitarianism: For and Against* (Section 3: "Two Cases" & Section 5: "Integrity"), Bernard Williams presents one of the most celebrated critiques of consequentialism in modern philosophy:

1. **The Thought Experiments:**
   - *George the Chemist:* George is an unemployed chemist in poor health with a struggling family. He is offered a lucrative job working on biological and chemical weapons. George has severe, lifelong moral objections to chemical warfare. If he refuses the job, another chemist who is zealous and eager will take it and pursue chemical warfare research with tremendous speed and dedication. Utilitarianism insists George *must* take the job, because doing so produces less harm than the eager zealot taking it.
   - *Jim and the Indians:* Jim wanders into a South American town where a military captain, Pedro, is about to execute twenty captive indigenous villagers. Pedro offers Jim a guest privilege: if Jim shoots one villager with his own hand, Pedro will release the other nineteen; if Jim refuses, Pedro’s soldiers will immediately execute all twenty. Utilitarianism asserts without hesitation that Jim *must* kill the innocent villager.
2. **The Critique: Negative Responsibility and Integrity:**
   - Utilitarianism is committed to **negative responsibility**: I am just as responsible for things that happen because I failed to prevent them as for things I intentionally cause directly.
   - Williams demonstrates that this leads to an absurd, soul-destroying result. A human being is not an impartial transmission belt for cosmic utility. Every human being has deep, identity-conferring "ground projects" and moral convictions.
   - To demand that George or Jim immediately discard their deepest convictions simply because someone else (Pedro) has created a bad state of affairs is to demand that the agent abandon their own **Integrity** (*die Integrität*). Utilitarianism alienates a person from their own actions, treating them as a mere calculator of consequences produced by others.

#### Distractor Analysis
- **Option A is incorrect:** Physics has no bearing on Williams’s moral-psychological critique.
- **Option C is incorrect:** It reduces a profound existential and psychological argument about moral agency and integrity to mundane accounting economics.
- **Option D is incorrect:** Williams is a thoroughly secular philosopher; his argument is grounded in moral psychology, agency, and the conditions of a meaningful human life.

**Primary Source / Textual Reference:** Bernard Williams, "A Critique of Utilitarianism," in J.J.C. Smart and Bernard Williams, *Utilitarianism: For and Against* (Cambridge University Press, 1973), Sections 3–5, pp. 93–118.
