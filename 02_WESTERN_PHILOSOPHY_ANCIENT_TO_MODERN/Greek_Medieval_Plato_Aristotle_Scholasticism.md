### Question 1: Presocratic Metaphysics (Heraclitean Flux vs. Eleatic Monism)
**Format**: Assertion-Reason (Format B)

**Assertion (A)**: Parmenides of Elea asserts in *On Nature* (Fragment B8) that "What Is" (*to eon*) must be ungenerated (*ageneton*), imperishable (*anolethron*), indivisible (*oude diaireton*), and completely motionless (*atremes*), strictly denying the reality of qualitative change and spatial locomotion.  
**Reason (R)**: For Parmenides, all generation (*genesis*) and locomotion entail that what is not (*to me eon*) must be spoken of or conceived; but because non-being is strictly unthinkable and unutterable (*Fragment B2: "for neither could you know what is not... nor could you point it out"*), any transition from non-being to being or from place to empty place involves a logical self-contradiction.

**Options**:
- (A) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the strictly correct logical and metaphysical explanation of (A).
- (B) Both (A) and (R) are true, but (R) is merely an epistemological commentary and not the metaphysical grounding of (A).
- (C) (A) is true, but (R) is false because Parmenides grounds the immobility of Being on empirical astronomical observations in the *Way of Seeming* (*Doxa*).
- (D) (A) is false because Parmenides, like Heraclitus, accepted perpetual physical flux at the sensory level while reserving unity solely for divine *Logos*.

**Correct Answer**: **(A)**

#### Philosophical Grounding & Demonstration:
In Eleatic metaphysics, Parmenides formulates the first strict deductive ontological logic in Western thought. In the *Way of Truth* (*Aletheia*), the goddess sets forth two conceivable paths of inquiry: the path that "it is and cannot not-be" (*hopos estin te kai hos ouk esti me einai*) and the path that "it is not and that it is necessary that it not-be" (*hos ouk estin te kai hos chreon esti me einai*). The second path is dismissed as an utterly impossible way of inquiry because non-being (*to me eon*) cannot be cognized or articulated (Fragment B2). 

Parmenides deduces the attributes (*semata*) of Being directly from this principle in Fragment B8:
1. *Ungenerated and Imperishable*: If Being came to be, it must have come from non-being (which is impossible, since non-being is nothing and has no causal efficacy: *ex nihilo nihil fit*) or from Being (in which case it already was).
2. *Indivisible and Continuous*: There is no "non-being" (void or gap) interspersed between portions of Being that could separate one part from another (*all' homou pan estin*, "all together it is one, continuous").
3. *Motionless*: Locomotion requires empty space (void) into which an entity moves; but void is non-being, which does not exist. 

Hence, (R) provides the exact deductive engine that compels the radical monistic conclusion formulated in (A).

#### Distractor Analysis:
- **(B) is incorrect**: (R) is not a disconnected or purely subjective psychological remark; in Parmenidean philosophy, thinking and being are co-extensive (*to gar auto noein estin te kai einai*, Fragment B3). The unthinkability of non-being is identical with its ontological impossibility; thus (R) is the direct metaphysical grounding of (A).
- **(C) is incorrect**: Parmenides explicitly separates the truth deduced by rational deduction (*logos*) in the *Way of Truth* from the deceitful cosmos of mortal opinions (*Doxa*). The attributes of *to eon* are deduced via *a priori* reductio arguments, not astronomical observation.
- **(D) is incorrect**: Parmenides never accepts flux as real Being; the sensory realm of change is denounced as mortal delusion (*doxai broteiai*) rooted in naming two opposites (Light and Night) instead of the singular One. Conflating Parmenides with Heraclitus violates the Eleatic rejection of Becoming.

**Primary Textual Reference**: Parmenides, *Fragments* (Diels-Kranz 28 B2, B3, B8); Kirk, Raven, and Schofield, *The Presocratic Philosophers*, Cambridge University Press.

### Question 2: Eleatic Paradoxes and Aristotle's Critique of Infinity
**Format**: Multi-Statement Evaluation / MSQ (Format C)

Consider the following statements regarding Zeno's paradox of motion (specifically the *Dichotomy* and *Achilles and the Tortoise*) and Aristotle's resolution in *Physics* (Books VI and VIII):

1. Zeno's *Dichotomy* argues that a runner cannot traverse a stadium because before reaching the endpoint, the runner must reach the midpoint, and before that, the midpoint of the remainder *ad infinitum*, requiring an infinite number of spatial acts to be performed in a finite time.
2. Aristotle in *Physics* Book VI initially resolves the paradox by distinguishing between two senses of infinity: infinity with respect to division (*kata diairesin*) and infinity with respect to extremities (*tois eschatois*), arguing that both the spatial interval and the temporal duration are infinitely divisible in the same manner.
3. Aristotle in *Physics* Book VIII realizes his Book VI solution is insufficient because it merely asserts that time is divisible, without explaining how an actual infinite series can be traversed, thereby introducing the vital metaphysical distinction between *potential infinity* (*kata dynamin*) and *actual infinity* (*kat' energeian*).
4. Aristotle holds that when a runner traverses a continuous distance without stopping, the midpoints exist *actually* as independent markers, thereby proving that an actual infinite can be successfully completed.

Which of the above statements are strictly correct?
- (A) 1, 2, and 3 only
- (B) 1 and 4 only
- (C) 2, 3, and 4 only
- (D) 1, 2, 3, and 4

**Correct Answer**: **(A)**

#### Philosophical Grounding & Demonstration:
Zeno of Elea designed his paradoxes to defend Parmenides' thesis that plurality and motion are self-contradictory illusions. 
- *Statement 1 is correct*: The *Dichotomy* (or *Runners*) demonstrates that motion cannot even begin (or finish) because traversing any continuous magnitude requires completing an infinite progression of sub-intervals ($1/2, 1/4, 1/8 \dots$ or $1/2, 3/4, 7/8 \dots$) in a finite time.
- *Statement 2 is correct*: In *Physics* VI.2 (233a13-31), Aristotle responds by pointing out an equivocation in Zeno's definition of "finite time." An interval is finite with respect to its extremities, but infinitely divisible within itself. Since time is continuous in the exact same sense as space, a runner traverses an infinitely divisible space in an infinitely divisible time.
- *Statement 3 is correct*: In *Physics* VIII.8 (263a11-b9), Aristotle candidly admits that the solution in Book VI is an *ad hominem* or dialectical reply that does not reach the core metaphysical problem: even if time is divisible, how can a mind or body exhaust an infinite number of parts? Aristotle's definitive answer is that the continuous line is only *potentially* infinite in division (*kata dynamin*), not *actually* infinite (*kat' energeian*).
- *Statement 4 is incorrect*: Aristotle explicitly denies that the continuous trajectory possesses actual parts during uninterrupted motion. For a point on a line to be actualized (*energeia*), the runner must either pause, turn around, or mentally register it as a distinct starting point and endpoint (*arche kai teleute*). Continuous motion treats the points merely as potential divisions; hence the runner does NOT traverse an actual infinity.

#### Distractor Analysis:
- Statements 1, 2, and 3 accurately capture the progressive development of Aristotle's mathematical and metaphysical physics.
- Statement 4 contains a profound conceptual reversal: if Aristotle held that midpoints exist *actually* during uninterrupted locomotion, he would fall directly into Zeno's trap (admitting that an actual infinity is completed, which Aristotle holds is impossible: *Physics* 263a6).

**Primary Textual Reference**: Aristotle, *Physics*, Book VI, ch. 2 (233a) and Book VIII, ch. 8 (263a-b); Jonathan Barnes (ed.), *The Complete Works of Aristotle*, Princeton University Press.

### Question 3: Plato's Parmenides and the Third Man Argument (TMA)
**Format**: Conceptual Multiple-Choice (Format A)

In Plato's *Parmenides* (132a-b), Parmenides formulates the classical objection against the Theory of Forms famously dubbed by Aristotle as the "Third Man Argument" (*Tritos Anthropos*). Following Gregory Vlastos's celebrated formalization, the infinite regress generated by the argument gets off the ground only because Socrates inadvertently accepts which of the following pairs of tacit premises?

- (A) The Theory of Flux (Heraclitus) and the Relationality of Predicates.
- (B) The Self-Predication Assumption (SP: The Form of Large is itself large) and the Non-Identity Assumption (NI: If a set of things are all F, the Form in virtue of which they are F cannot be identical with any of the members of that set).
- (C) The Principle of Sufficient Reason (PSR) and the Indivisibility of the Monad.
- (D) The Epistemic Parity Assumption and the Inherence Theory of Accidents.

**Correct Answer**: **(B)**

#### Philosophical Grounding & Demonstration:
In *Parmenides* 132a1-b2, Parmenides interrogates young Socrates:
1. When you see a collection of large things ($a, b, c$), you perceive one single Form, Largeness ($L_1$), by virtue of which they all appear large (the "One-over-Many" premise: OM).
2. Parmenides then asks: What if you consider the Form of Largeness ($L_1$) itself alongside the original large particulars ($a, b, c$)? Will there not appear another single Form of Largeness ($L_2$) over both the particulars and the first Form?
3. This generates an infinite regress of Forms ($L_1, L_2, L_3 \dots$), contradicting the fundamental Platonic axiom that each Form is unique (*monoeides*).

In his seminal paper ("The Third Man Argument in the *Parmenides*", 1954), Gregory Vlastos proved that the regress is logically valid only if two unstated assumptions are operating in Socrates' mindset:
- **Self-Predication (SP)**: Any Form $F$-ness is itself an exemplary instance of $F$ (e.g., Largeness is itself large; Justice is itself just: *Phaedo* 100c, *Protagoras* 330c-d).
- **Non-Identity (NI)**: If any set of entities $x_1, x_2 \dots x_n$ are $F$, the Form $F$-ness in virtue of which they are $F$ cannot be identical to any of $x_1, x_2 \dots x_n$.

Because $L_1$ is itself large (by SP), we form a new set $\{a, b, c, L_1\}$. By Non-Identity (NI), the Form $L_2$ in virtue of which this new set is large cannot be identical to $L_1$ (nor to $a, b, c$). Hence, a second Form $L_2$ is born, and the iteration never terminates.

#### Distractor Analysis:
- **(A) is incorrect**: The TMA does not depend on Heraclitean flux; it is an internal critique of Plato's logical-metaphysical ontology of transcendent Forms.
- **(C) is incorrect**: The Principle of Sufficient Reason and Monadology are Leibnizian concepts of the 17th century; they play no role in the Greek logic of the *Parmenides*.
- **(D) is incorrect**: Aristotle's accident theory belongs to his *Categories*; Plato's problem in the TMA stems directly from the hypostatization of the Form as an ideal particular that participates in its own property.

**Primary Textual Reference**: Plato, *Parmenides*, 132a-133a; Gregory Vlastos, "The Third Man Argument in the Parmenides", *Philosophical Review*, 63(3), 1954, pp. 319-349.

### Question 4: Plato's Epistemology in the *Theaetetus*
**Format**: Multi-Statement Evaluation / MSQ (Format C)

In Plato's *Theaetetus*, Socrates conducts a rigorous cross-examination (*elenchus*) of Theaetetus's three successive definitions of Knowledge (*episteme*). Evaluate the validity of the following statements regarding the dialogue:

1. The first definition—that knowledge is Perception (*aisthesis*)—is shown by Socrates to be ontologically and epistemologically tied to Protagoras's relativism ("man is the measure of all things") and Heraclitus's doctrine of radical flux.
2. Socrates refutes the identity of knowledge with perception by demonstrating that common categorical concepts such as existence (*ousia*), sameness (*tauton*), difference (*heteron*), and number cannot be perceived by the bodily senses, but are grasped by the soul herself functioning through herself (*aute di' hautes*).
3. The second definition—that knowledge is True Judgment/Belief (*orthe doxa*)—fails because an advocate or orator in a court of law can persuade a jury into holding a true belief about a robbery they did not witness, without the jury possessing genuine knowledge.
4. The third definition—that knowledge is True Judgment with an Account/Logos (*meta logou alethe doxan*)—is ultimately vindicated by Socrates in the closing pages of the dialogue when *logos* is defined as grasping the specific difference (*diaphora*) of the object.

Which of the statements given above are strictly correct?
- (A) 1, 2, and 3 only
- (B) 2 and 4 only
- (C) 1, 3, and 4 only
- (D) 1, 2, 3, and 4

**Correct Answer**: **(A)**

#### Philosophical Grounding & Demonstration:
- *Statement 1 is correct*: In *Theaetetus* 151e-160e, Socrates synthesizes Theaetetus's equation of knowledge with perception with Protagorean subjectivism (*homo-mensura*) and the Heraclitean ontology that all is becoming, showing that if knowledge is perception, truth is reduced to instantaneous, private, infallible appearances where contradiction is impossible.
- *Statement 2 is correct*: In *Theaetetus* 184b-186e (the famous *koiná* passage), Socrates shows that the senses are merely instruments *through* which (*di' hou*) we perceive colours or sounds, but properties common to diverse senses (being, non-being, likeness, unlikeness, identity, difference, unity) are grasped by the soul acting independently of bodily sense organs. Since knowledge requires reaching truth, and truth requires grasping being (*ousia*), perception without the soul's rational judgment cannot constitute knowledge.
- *Statement 3 is correct*: In *Theaetetus* 201a-c, Socrates introduces the courtroom analogy. Persuasive trial lawyers convince jurors of true facts; the jurors hold true belief (*alethe doxa*), yet only an eyewitness possesses *episteme*. True belief is therefore distinct from knowledge.
- *Statement 4 is incorrect*: The *Theaetetus* is famously aporetic. In 206c-210b, Socrates examines three possible definitions of *logos*:
  1. *Logos* as vocalizing thought with verbs and nouns (rejected as trivial; anyone who is not deaf or mute does this).
  2. *Logos* as enumeration of elements (rejected; one can spell a syllable correctly by memory without understanding its underlying phonetic principles, as shown in the dream of Socrates).
  3. *Logos* as giving the distinguishing mark or difference (*diaphora*) (rejected as viciously circular: to add a "rational account of the difference" to a true belief about $X$ means either we already *know* the difference—making the definition of knowledge presuppose knowledge—or we merely hold a *belief* about the difference, which leaves us back at mere true belief). Thus, the definition fails, and the dialogue ends in aporia (210b-d).

#### Distractor Analysis:
- Since Statement 4 is historically and textually false (the dialogue ends without solving the definition of knowledge, leaving the *Theaetetus* as the classical refutation of the simple JTB model avant la lettre), options (B), (C), and (D) are rejected. Only (A) is correct.

**Primary Textual Reference**: Plato, *Theaetetus*, 184b-186e, 201a-210d; John McDowell (trans. & comm.), *Plato: Theaetetus*, Oxford University Press.

### Question 5: Plato's Divided Line and the Epistemic Shift from *Dianoia* to *Noesis*
**Format**: Conceptual Multiple-Choice (Format A)

In Book VI of the *Republic* (509d-511e), Plato uses the Line Analogy (*Gramme*) to map cognitive states (*pathemata en te psyche*) to ontological realms. Which of the following statements precisely captures the distinction between the third subsection (*Dianoia* / Thought) and the fourth subsection (*Noesis* / Understanding or Intellection)?

- (A) *Dianoia* deals exclusively with sensible particulars using empirical induction, whereas *Noesis* deals with physical shadows using mathematical deductions.
- (B) In *Dianoia*, the soul is forced to use sensible physical diagrams (such as visible geometric triangles) as images and treats its foundational axioms as unexamined starting hypotheses (*hypotheseis*), moving downwards to a deductive conclusion; whereas in *Noesis*, the soul proceeds from hypotheses purely through dialectic without any sensible images, ascending upward to an unhypothetical first principle (*arche anypothetos*).
- (C) *Dianoia* yields infallible knowledge of the Form of the Good directly, whereas *Noesis* is an inferior, discursive faculty limited to hypothetical syllogisms.
- (D) *Dianoia* belongs to the Visible realm (*horaton*) and is characterized by *pistis*, whereas *Noesis* is the only subsection belonging to the Intelligible realm (*noeton*).

**Correct Answer**: **(B)**

#### Philosophical Grounding & Demonstration:
In the Divided Line (509d-511e), Plato bisects reality into the Visible (*horaton*) and the Intelligible (*noeton*), and then subdivides each in the same ratio:
- **Visible Realm**:
  1. *Eikasia* (Imagination/Conjecture): Shadows, reflections, images of physical objects.
  2. *Pistis* (Belief/Confidence): Actual physical animals, plants, artefacts.
- **Intelligible Realm**:
  3. *Dianoia* (Discursive Thought / Mathematical reasoning): The geometer makes use of visible figures (drawing a square on sand), treating them as likenesses of the ideal Square itself. Crucially, the geometer takes mathematical postulates (odd/even, three kinds of angles) as unquestioned "hypotheses" (*hypotheseis*) and moves *downward* (*epi teleuten*) to deduce theorems, never inquiring into the ultimate justification of those initial axioms.
  4. *Noesis* (Dialectical Understanding): In dialectic, reason does not treat hypotheses as bedrock truths, but strictly as stepping-stones (*epibaseis kai hormas*). It uses them to ascend *upward* to that which is beyond hypothesis: the Unhypothetical First Principle of everything (*to ep' archen anypotheton tou pantos*), identified with the Form of the Good (*tou agathou idea*). Once this principle is grasped, reason descends again through pure Forms, without leaning on a single sensible image (*aistheton oudeni proschromenos*).

#### Distractor Analysis:
- **(A) is incorrect**: *Dianoia* belongs to the intelligible realm (*noeton*), not the sensible realm; it contemplates intelligible mathematicals, even if it employs sensible drawings as aids.
- **(C) is incorrect**: This completely reverses the epistemic hierarchy. It is *Noesis* that grasps the Form of the Good; *Dianoia* cannot ascend past its initial hypotheses.
- **(D) is incorrect**: Both *Dianoia* and *Noesis* reside within the upper, Intelligible portion of the divided line.

**Primary Textual Reference**: Plato, *Republic*, Book VI, 509d-511e; F.M. Cornford, *The Republic of Plato*, Oxford University Press.

### Question 6: Aristotle on Substance: *Categories* vs. *Metaphysics* Zeta
**Format**: Conceptual Multiple-Choice (Format A)

A major tension in Aristotelian scholarship centers on the ontological status of **Substance** (*ousia*). Which of the following options correctly articulates the doctrinal shift between the *Categories* (Chapter 5) and *Metaphysics* Book Zeta ($\text{Z}$)?

- (A) In the *Categories*, Aristotle asserts that Universal Forms are Primary Substances; in *Metaphysics* Zeta, he embraces Platonic transcendence (*chorismos*) and rejects matter entirely.
- (B) In the *Categories*, the individual composite particular (e.g., this individual man or this individual horse) is unequivocally designated as Primary Substance (*prote ousia*) because it is the ultimate subject of all predication and inherence; whereas in *Metaphysics* Zeta, Aristotle investigates what makes a substance a substance, rejecting the mere composite (*synolon*) and prime matter (*hyle*) as primary, and concludes that Form (*eidos*) / Essence (*to ti en einai*) has the primary claim to being substance.
- (C) In the *Categories*, Aristotle denies that species and genera have any secondary reality; in *Metaphysics* Zeta, he renames genera as Prime Matter.
- (D) In the *Categories*, accident is identical to essence; in *Metaphysics* Zeta, Aristotle abandons the concept of *ousia* in favor of Heraclitean flux.

**Correct Answer**: **(B)**

#### Philosophical Grounding & Demonstration:
In *Categories* ch. 5 (2a11-19), Aristotle establishes the criterion of substance based on linguistic and logical predication:
> "Substance, in the truest and primary and most definite sense of the word, is that which is neither predicable of a subject nor present in a subject; for instance, the individual man or horse."
Here, the concrete particular (*tode ti*) is Primary Substance (*prote ousia*), while species and genera (*anthropos*, *zoon*) are Secondary Substances (*deuterai ousiai*).

However, in *Metaphysics* Book Zeta ($\text{Z}$), Aristotle interrogates the underlying metaphysical ground of being (*to on he on*). He tests four candidates for *ousia*:
1. The Substratum (*hypokeimenon*): If the substratum is stripped of all predicates, we arrive at Prime Matter (*prote hyle*), which lacks determinacy and separability (*choriston kai tode ti*), and therefore cannot be primary substance (Z.3, 1029a27-30).
2. The Composite of form and matter (*synolon*): The concrete individual is posterior to its constituents and subject to generation and corruption.
3. The Universal (*katholou*): In Z.13 (1038b8-15), Aristotle explicitly proves that *no universal can be a substance*, because substance is peculiar to that which has it and not shared by many.
4. The Essence / Form (*to ti en einai* / *eidos*): Aristotle concludes in Z.17 that Form is the primary cause of being in a thing—it is the structural, actualizing principle that organizes matter into a unified, intelligible substance. Hence, *eidos* takes precedence as Primary Substance in the fundamental metaphysical ontology of *Metaphysics* Zeta.

#### Distractor Analysis:
- **(A) is incorrect**: Aristotle never adopts Platonic transcendence (*chorismos*); his forms are immanent (*enhyloi logoi*), not separate entities dwelling in a transcendent heaven.
- **(C) is incorrect**: The *Categories* explicitly acknowledges species and genera as Secondary Substances (*deuterai ousiai*, 2a14); neither are they equated to Prime Matter in Zeta.
- **(D) is incorrect**: Aristotle maintains a sharp distinction between accidental predication (*kata symbebekos*) and essential predication (*kath' hauto*) in both works; he never endorses Heraclitean flux.

**Primary Textual Reference**: Aristotle, *Categories*, ch. 5 (2a11-2b22); *Metaphysics*, Book Zeta ($\text{Z}$), ch. 1-3, 13, 17; Michael Frede and Günther Patzig, *Aristoteles 'Metaphysik Z'*, C.H. Beck.

### Question 7: Aristotle's Four Causes, *Energeia*, and the Unmoved Mover
**Format**: Multi-Statement Evaluation / MSQ (Format C)

Examine the following statements regarding Aristotle's teleology, metaphysics of change, and theology in *Physics* II and *Metaphysics* Lambda ($\Lambda$):

1. For Aristotle, the Final Cause (*telos*) in natural organisms frequently coincides in number and definition with both the Formal Cause (*eidos*) and the Efficient Cause (*arche kineseos*), such that the essence of a human being is simultaneously its developmental goal and the generative source (a human generates a human).
2. *Dynamis* (potentiality) is strictly prior to *Energeia* (actuality) in every respect—ontologically, temporally, and epistemologically—because an acorn must exist before the mature oak tree can emerge.
3. In *Metaphysics* Book Lambda, the Prime Mover (Unmoved Mover) moves the cosmos not as an efficient, pushing cause (*aition kinetikon*), but purely as a final cause (*hos eromenon* / as an object of love and desire), without undergoing any change or possessing potentiality.
4. Because the Unmoved Mover is pure actuality (*pure energeia*) devoid of matter, its eternal cognitive activity is described as a "thinking of thinking" (*noesis noeseos noesis*), since thinking any contingent object would imply passivity and potentiality.

Which of the statements given above are strictly correct?
- (A) 1 and 2 only
- (B) 1, 3, and 4 only
- (C) 2, 3, and 4 only
- (D) 1, 2, 3, and 4

**Correct Answer**: **(B)**

#### Philosophical Grounding & Demonstration:
- *Statement 1 is correct*: In *Physics* II.7 (198a24-27), Aristotle states that the formal, final, and efficient causes often run together into one (*eis hen erchetai to eidos kai to hou heneka kai to hothen he kinesis*): the form of the adult human is the end toward which the embryo develops (*telos*), and this form was present in the parent who initiated generation (*efficient cause*).
- *Statement 2 is incorrect*: In *Metaphysics* Theta ($\Theta$.8, 1049b4-1051a2), Aristotle systematically proves the **Priority of Actuality over Potentiality** (*proteron energeia physei kai ousiai*):
  - *In Definition/Epistemology*: We know potentiality only through its corresponding actuality (we define "builder" as one who can build).
  - *In Time (generically)*: Though in a particular individual the seed precedes the plant, an actual plant was necessary to produce the seed.
  - *In Substance/Ontology*: The actual is the end (*telos*) for the sake of which the potential exists; eternal things are prior to perishable ones, and eternal things contain no potentiality to not-be.
- *Statement 3 is correct*: In *Metaphysics* $\Lambda$.7 (1072a26-b4), Aristotle demonstrates that the primary mover must produce eternal, continuous motion without being moved itself. It moves as the desirable and intelligible move: *kinei de hos eromenon* (it moves by being loved/desired). If it acted as an efficient cause involving mechanical contact, it would suffer reaction and change.
- *Statement 4 is correct*: In *Metaphysics* $\Lambda$.9 (1074b15-35), Aristotle examines the divine intellect (*nous*). If it thought something external, its value would depend on that object, and it would involve passive transition from potentiality to actuality. Therefore, divine thought must be entirely self-contained: its thinking is the thinking of thinking (*esti he noesis noeseos noesis*).

#### Distractor Analysis:
- Since Statement 2 contradicts Aristotle's central thesis in *Metaphysics* $\Theta$.8 (Priority of Actuality), any option including Statement 2 ((A), (C), and (D)) is categorically false. Only (B) is sound.

**Primary Textual Reference**: Aristotle, *Physics* II.7; *Metaphysics* Book $\Theta$, ch. 8; *Metaphysics* Book $\Lambda$, ch. 6-9; W.D. Ross, *Aristotle's Metaphysics*, Oxford Clarendon Press.

### Question 8: Aristotle's Moral Psychology: *Akrasia* and the Practical Syllogism
**Format**: Conceptual Multiple-Choice (Format A)

In *Nicomachean Ethics* Book VII (Chapters 1-3), Aristotle addresses the problem of **Akrasia** (incontinence / weakness of will), navigating between the Socratic denial that anyone errs knowingly and the common sense observation that agents often act contrary to their better judgment. How does Aristotle explain the precise cognitive failure of the akratic agent using the mechanics of the **Practical Syllogism**?

- (A) The akratic agent undergoes a total permanent destruction of the rational soul, becoming an animal driven solely by non-rational appetites.
- (B) The agent possesses the universal premise of the practical syllogism (e.g., "Tasting excessive sweets is harmful") and the appetite is dormant; however, the agent calculates that pleasure is the ultimate good and consciously rejects temperance.
- (C) The agent possesses both the universal premise prohibiting the act and a competing universal premise sparked by appetite (e.g., "Everything sweet is pleasant"); under the influence of bodily passion, the agent's grasp of the *particular minor premise* (e.g., "This particular object is excessively sweet/harmful") is either absent or held only in a dormant, non-active state—akin to someone who is asleep, mad, or reciting verses of Empedocles while drunk.
- (D) The agent is coerced by external physical force (*bia*), completely eliminating all moral responsibility (*akousion*).

**Correct Answer**: **(C)**

#### Philosophical Grounding & Demonstration:
In *Protagoras* (352b-c), Socrates famously maintained that knowledge is a ruler (*hegemonikon*): if someone knows what is best, they will inevitably do it; akrasia is merely ignorance (*amathia*).

In *Nicomachean Ethics* VII.3 (1146b31-1147b19), Aristotle refines this through his distinction between possessing knowledge (*echein*) and using/activating knowledge (*theorein/energein*). In moral reasoning, actions are concluded through a practical syllogism containing:
1. A **Universal Major Premise** of reason: *Everything sweet and unhealthy must not be tasted*.
2. A competing **Universal Premise** of desire/appetite: *Everything sweet is pleasant*.
3. A **Particular Minor Premise**: *This thing before me is sweet and unhealthy*.

When appetite (*epithymia*) is intensely active, it compels the agent to focus exclusively on the premise of pleasure. The agent may still utter words expressing moral knowledge (e.g., "This is bad for me"), but Aristotle notes that this is like an actor on a stage or a drunk person reciting poetry: the knowledge is not interiorized or active (*ouk esti de to echein auto alla to legein*). Crucially, the *minor premise* (which concerns sensible particulars that trigger immediate physical action) is overpowered by passion. Thus, Socrates was partially right: full, active knowledge in the strict sense (*kyrios episteme*) is not dragged about by passion; rather, it is perceptual/particular awareness that is eclipsed.

#### Distractor Analysis:
- **(A) is incorrect**: Aristotle explicitly distinguishes the akratic (*akrates*) from the vicious or intemperate person (*akolastos*). The intemperate person has corrupted reason and acts on deliberate principle (*prohairesis*), believing pleasure should always be pursued; the akratic retains the correct general principles but fails in application.
- **(B) is incorrect**: This describes the *akolastos* (the thoroughly vicious man), not the *akrates*.
- **(D) is incorrect**: Aristotle notes in *NE* III.1 that acts done out of bodily appetite or passion are voluntary (*hekousion*), not compelled by physical force from without (*bia*).

**Primary Textual Reference**: Aristotle, *Nicomachean Ethics*, Book VII, ch. 1-3 (1146b8-1147b19); Sarah Broadie, *Ethics with Aristotle*, Oxford University Press.

### Question 9: Augustine on the Ontology of Evil and the Phenomenological Distension of Time
**Format**: Assertion-Reason (Format B)

**Assertion (A)**: In *Confessions* (Book XI) and *De Civitate Dei* (Book XI), St. Augustine resolves the Problem of Evil without compromising the omnipotence and omnibenevolence of God by defining evil (*malum*) not as an ontological nature, substance, or positive reality (*substantia*), but strictly as a privation of the good (*privatio boni*) or a disorder of the will (*aversio a Deo*).  
**Reason (R)**: In Augustine's ontology, God created all things *ex nihilo*, and insofar as any entity exists, it possesses measure, form, and order (*modus, species, ordo*), and is therefore intrinsically good; consequently, evil cannot be a self-subsisting metaphysical principle (as the Manichaeans claimed), but exists only as a parasitic defect, corruption, or lack of due perfection in a good nature.

**Options**:
- (A) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the strictly correct foundational metaphysical explanation of (A).
- (B) Both (A) and (R) are true, but (R) is an ethical evaluation that does not explain the ontological claim in (A).
- (C) (A) is true, but (R) is false because Augustine retained the Manichaean dualism of light and darkness until the end of his life.
- (D) (A) is false because Augustine argued in *De Libero Arbitrio* that God created evil as an aesthetic contrast to highlight divine justice.

**Correct Answer**: **(A)**

#### Philosophical Grounding & Demonstration:
Augustine's resolution of the Problem of Evil was formulated directly in response to his nine years as a Manichaean "Hearer". Manichaeism posited two co-eternal, opposed cosmic substances: the Kingdom of Light (Good) and the Kingdom of Darkness (Evil).

Under the influence of Neo-Platonism (specifically Plotinus's *Enneads* I.8), Augustine realized that Being and Goodness are convertible (*ens et bonum convertuntur*). 
- In *De Natura Boni* (ch. 1-4) and *Confessions* VII.12, Augustine argues that everything that exists was created by God *ex nihilo*. Insofar as a thing has being, it participates in goodness because it exhibits measure, form, and order (*modus, species, et ordo*).
- Evil has no positive essence (*nulla natura est malum*); it is nothing other than the corruption (*corruptio*) or diminution of goodness. Complete corruption would eradicate all being entirely.
- Moral evil arises through the free choice of the will (*liberum arbitrium voluntatis*), which is not a turning toward an evil substance, but a defective turning away (*aversio*) from the higher, immutable Good (God) toward lower, mutable goods (*conversio ad creaturam*).

Hence, (R) provides the exact ontological framework that explains and sustains (A).

#### Distractor Analysis:
- **(B) is incorrect**: (R) is fundamentally ontological and cosmological (dealing with *creatio ex nihilo*, being, and substance), not merely ethical.
- **(C) is incorrect**: Augustine decisively broke with Manichaeism in Milan around 386 CE, explicitly dedicating treatises (*Contra Faustum*, *De Natura Boni*) to refuting dualism.
- **(D) is incorrect**: Augustine strictly denies that God created evil; God permits evil that arises from the defect of created free will and integrates its consequences into universal justice, but evil itself has no efficient cause—only a *deficient cause* (*causa deficiens*: *De Civitate Dei* XII.7).

**Primary Textual Reference**: St. Augustine, *Confessions*, Book VII, ch. 12-16; Book XI; *De Natura Boni*, ch. 1-10; *De Civitate Dei*, Book XI, ch. 9, 22; Book XII, ch. 7.

### Question 10: Anselm's Ontological Argument and the Gaunilo Debate
**Format**: Multi-Statement Evaluation / MSQ (Format C)

Examine the logical architecture of St. Anselm's ontological arguments in *Proslogion* (Chapters II and III) and his response to the Benedictine monk Gaunilo of Marmoutiers (*In Behalf of the Fool*):

1. In *Proslogion* II, Anselm defines God as "that than which nothing greater can be conceived" (*aliquid quo nihil maius cogitari possit*), arguing that if this exists solely in the intellect (*in intellectu*), it could be conceived to exist also in reality (*in re*), which would be greater; hence, to remain that than which nothing greater can be conceived, it must exist *in re*.
2. In *In Behalf of the Fool*, Gaunilo attempts a *reductio ad absurdum* by substituting God with the "Lost Island" (*insula perdita*), alleging that by Anselm's logic, an island more excellent than all other lands must exist in reality simply because we can conceive of it.
3. In his *Liber Apologeticus contra Gaunilonem*, Anselm concedes Gaunilo's critique for contingent entities, but demonstrates that his argument applies exclusively to *that than which nothing greater can be conceived*, because an island has a beginning, an end, and parts, and can easily be conceived not to exist, whereas God alone is conceived as possessing *necessary existence* (*Proslogion* III: that which cannot even be conceived not to exist).
4. Norman Malcolm and Charles Hartshorne argue that Anselm presented two logically distinct arguments: an invalid argument in *Proslogion* II based on existence as a property/predicate, and a valid modal ontological argument in *Proslogion* III based on the impossibility of non-existence (necessary existence).

Which of the statements given above are strictly correct?
- (A) 1 and 2 only
- (B) 2 and 3 only
- (C) 1, 2, 3, and 4
- (D) 1, 3, and 4 only

**Correct Answer**: **(C)**

#### Philosophical Grounding & Demonstration:
- *Statement 1 is correct*: *Proslogion* II operates on the comparison between existence in the mind alone (*in solo intellectu*) and existence in objective reality (*in re*). Since real existence is greater, if the definition existed only in the mind, a greater could be conceived (namely, the same entity existing *in re*). By reductio, the Fool is forced to admit that God exists in reality.
- *Statement 2 is correct*: Gaunilo's critique (sec. 6) uses the parallel of a lost island containing inestimable riches. If someone describes it, it exists in our intellect; but if that person concludes it must exist in reality because it is superior to all real lands, we would consider him a fool.
- *Statement 3 is correct*: Anselm's reply (*Apologeticus*, ch. 3-5) is a masterclass in modal distinction. He tells Gaunilo: *"If anyone can find for me anything existing either in reality or in concept alone, except 'that than which a greater cannot be conceived', to which the logic of my argument can apply, I will find and give him that Lost Island, never to be lost again."* The concept of an island is inherently contingent—it has boundaries, material compositions, and can be conceived not to exist. The concept of God, however, is unique: if conceived at all, it must be conceived as having neither beginning nor end, i.e., having necessary existence.
- *Statement 4 is correct*: In his landmark 1960 article ("Anselm's Ontological Arguments", *Philosophical Review*), Norman Malcolm resurrected Anselm's argument by demonstrating that while Kant correctly refuted *Proslogion* II (showing existence is not a real predicate), *Proslogion* III does not treat bare existence as a predicate, but rather **necessary existence** (*that which cannot be conceived not to exist* vs *that which can be conceived not to exist*), which is an authentic perfection and modal property.

#### Distractor Analysis:
- All four statements are historically, logically, and textually accurate representations of the Anselmian corpus and its modern philosophical rehabilitation. Therefore, (C) is the only correct selection.

**Primary Textual Reference**: St. Anselm, *Proslogion*, Chapters II and III; Gaunilo, *Pro Insipiente*; Anselm, *Responsio ad Gaunilonem*; Norman Malcolm, "Anselm's Ontological Arguments", *The Philosophical Review*, 69(1), 1960, pp. 41-62.

### Question 11: Aquinas: The Five Ways and the Analogy of Being (*Analogia Entis*)
**Format**: Conceptual Multiple-Choice (Format A)

In *Summa Theologiae* (First Part, Question 2, Article 3 and Question 13, Article 5), St. Thomas Aquinas constructs the Five Ways (*Quinque Viae*) and the Doctrine of Analogy. Which of the following formulations precisely expresses Aquinas's technical distinction between causal series and the semantic predication of divine names?

- (A) The Five Ways depend on rejecting an infinite regress in an accidental causal series (*series causaliter per accidens*), while divine attributes are predicated *univocally* of God and creatures.
- (B) The Five Ways reject an infinite regress strictly within an essentially ordered causal series (*series causaliter per se*), where intermediate causes operate simultaneously and depend essentially on a primary cause; furthermore, divine names (such as "wise" or "good") are predicated of God and creatures neither *univocally* (which would reduce God to the genus of creatures) nor *purely equivocally* (which would lead to agnosticism), but *analogically* according to the analogy of proportionality and attribution.
- (C) The Five Ways are *a priori* proofs patterned directly after Anselm's *Proslogion*, and Aquinas asserts that our language about God is strictly equivocal.
- (D) Aquinas accepts that an infinite temporal chain of parents and children (*per se* series) is metaphysically impossible, thereby proving that the world had a temporal beginning.

**Correct Answer**: **(B)**

#### Philosophical Grounding & Demonstration:
Aquinas's cosmological proofs (*Quinque Viae*) do not argue against an infinite temporal regress into the past. In *ST* Ia, q. 46, a. 2, Aquinas explicitly insists that whether the world had a temporal beginning cannot be demonstrated by philosophical reason alone, but is known only through divine revelation. 

The proofs rely on the metaphysical distinction between two types of causal regress:
1. **Accidentally Ordered Series (*per accidens*)**: A father generates a son, who generates another son. The causality of the son in generating does not depend on the father continuing to exist. Aquinas concedes that an infinite regress *per accidens* is not intrinsically impossible (*ST* Ia, q. 46, a. 2, ad 7).
2. **Essentially Ordered Series (*per se*)**: A hand moves a staff, which moves a stone. The staff has no power to move the stone except insofar as it is *simultaneously* moved by the hand. Here, all intermediate causes are merely instrumental; without a First Mover possessing unborrowed causal efficacy, no motion could occur at all. The Five Ways reject an infinite regress strictly in this *per se* sense.

Regarding divine names (*ST* Ia, q. 13, a. 5):
- If names were **univocal** (*univoca*), God and creatures would belong to the same genus, violating divine transcendence and simplicity.
- If names were **equivocal** (*aequivoca*), theological discourse would collapse into total skepticism and equivocation fallacy.
- Hence, names are predicated **analogically** (*analogice*): what the name signifies (*res significata*) belongs primarily to God as the source of being, though our human mode of signification (*modus significandi*) is derived from finite creatures.

#### Distractor Analysis:
- **(A) is incorrect**: Aquinas explicitly permits an infinite regress *per accidens* in philosophy and rejects univocity.
- **(C) is incorrect**: Aquinas famously rejected Anselm's ontological argument in *ST* Ia, q. 2, a. 1, arguing that we do not have an intuitive knowledge of God's essence; proofs must be *a posteriori* (from effects to cause: *demonstratio quia*).
- **(D) is incorrect**: An ancestral chain (parent-child) is the textbook example of a series *per accidens*, not *per se*; Aquinas denied that philosophy can prove the non-eternity of the universe.

**Primary Textual Reference**: St. Thomas Aquinas, *Summa Theologiae*, First Part (Ia), Question 2, Articles 1-3; Question 13, Article 5; Question 46, Article 2; F.C. Copleston, *Aquinas*, Penguin Books.

### Question 12: The Medieval Problem of Universals
**Format**: Doctrinal Matching & Conceptual Evaluation (Format D)

The Scholastic debate over the ontological status of **Universals** (*universalia*) originated from Porphyry's *Isagoge* (translated by Boethius). Match the following medieval philosophical positions on universals with their precise technical thesis and principal champion:

| Position | Philosophical Thesis | Principal Champion |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **1. Extreme Realism** | (i) Universals are real independent extra-mental entities existing essentially in each individual; the essence of "humanity" is wholly present in both Socrates and Plato, differentiated only by accidents. | (p) Peter Abelard |
| **2. Moderate Realism** | (ii) Universals have no ontological reality outside the mind; they are mere spoken words or vocal emissions (*flatus vocis*). | (q) William of Champeaux |
| **3. Conceptualism / Non-Realism** | (iii) Universals exist *in re* (in things) as immanent common natures, abstracted by the active intellect (*intellectus agens*) as concepts (*post rem*), and exist *ante rem* in the divine mind. | (r) Roscelin of Compiègne / Early Nominalists |
| **4. Strict Nominalism / Vocalism** | (iv) Universals are meaningful words/assertions (*sermones*) that signify real things through mental concepts, rejecting both substantial identity of essences in particulars and mere physical sound. | (s) Thomas Aquinas / Aristotle |

**Correct Matching Pair**:
- (A) 1-(i)-(q), 2-(iii)-(s), 3-(iv)-(p), 4-(ii)-(r)
- (B) 1-(iii)-(s), 2-(i)-(q), 3-(ii)-(r), 4-(iv)-(p)
- (C) 1-(i)-(q), 2-(iv)-(p), 3-(iii)-(s), 4-(ii)-(r)
- (D) 1-(ii)-(r), 2-(iii)-(s), 3-(iv)-(p), 4-(i)-(q)

**Correct Answer**: **(A)**

#### Philosophical Grounding & Demonstration:
1. **Extreme Realism (William of Champeaux)**: Before being defeated by Abelard, William held the "Identity of Essence" theory: the entire universal substance of humanity is simultaneously and identically present in Socrates and Plato; the differences between them are purely accidental variations. This matches **1-(i)-(q)**.
2. **Moderate Realism (Thomas Aquinas / Albert the Great)**: Following Aristotle, Aquinas articulated the threefold status of universals (*triplex status universalis*):
   - *Universale ante rem*: as archetypes in the divine intellect.
   - *Universale in re*: as the common specific nature instantiated in matter (e.g. human nature individualised by *materia signata quantitate*).
   - *Universale post rem*: as the universal concept abstracted by the human intellect (*intellectus agens*). This matches **2-(iii)-(s)**.
3. **Conceptualism / Semantic Non-Realism (Peter Abelard)**: Abelard dismantled Champeaux's realism (pointing out that if humanity is identically wholly present in Socrates and Plato, then when Socrates is in Rome and Plato in Athens, humanity is in two places at once; or if Socrates is bad and Plato good, contraries inhere in the exact same substance). Abelard also rejected Roscelin's crude vocalism. Abelard posited that a universal is neither a thing (*res*) nor a mere physical blast of air (*vox*), but a significant word (*nomen* / *sermo*) established by human convention to signify common characteristics grasped by abstraction. This matches **3-(iv)-(p)**.
4. **Strict Nominalism / Vocalism (Roscelin of Compiègne)**: Roscelin held that only individual substances exist; universals are nothing more than physical vocal sounds (*flatus vocis*), leading directly to his tritheism in theology. This matches **4-(ii)-(r)**.

#### Distractor Analysis:
- Option (A) is the only permutation that accurately tracks the historic scholastic confrontation documented in Abelard's *Historia Calamitatum* and Aquinas's *De Ente et Essentia*.

**Primary Textual Reference**: Peter Abelard, *Logica 'Ingredientibus'*; St. Thomas Aquinas, *De Ente et Essentia* (On Being and Essence), ch. 3-4; Paul Vincent Spade, *Five Texts on the Mediaeval Problem of Universals*, Hackett Publishing.
